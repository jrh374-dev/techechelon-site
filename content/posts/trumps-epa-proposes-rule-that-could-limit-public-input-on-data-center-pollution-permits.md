The Trump administration is weighing a rule change that would give states the authority to decide how much — if any — public participation is required when companies seek air pollution permits for new facilities, a move that could accelerate data center construction while reducing community oversight.

The Environmental Protection Agency held a public hearing on the proposed change on Wednesday, drawing testimony from environmental advocates and community organizers who warned the rollback would leave residents with little recourse as data center development expands across the country.

Under the Clean Air Act, companies building facilities that emit air pollution must obtain permits categorized as either "major" or "minor" sources depending on whether they meet or exceed federal pollutant thresholds. Major sources require extensive federal and state review, including mandatory public hearings. Minor sources face less scrutiny — and it is that category that increasingly covers the diesel generators and gas engines being used to power data centers.

Operators including xAI and Meta have used minor source permitting processes to build behind-the-meter gas plants at their facilities. Under current EPA rules, some degree of public participation is required even for minor source permits, but enforcement varies widely by state.

If finalized, the proposed rule "would put state and local agencies most familiar with local issues in the driver's seat to determine whether, when, and for how long to provide opportunities for public participation for proposed new minor sources and modifications," an EPA spokesperson said, adding that the rule would not alter emissions standards themselves.

Critics argue that framing obscures real consequences for communities near planned facilities. "As someone actively working in communities with data centers, I know this to be fundamentally true: People want to have a say," said Vanessa Lynch, a Pennsylvania organizer with Moms Clean Air Force, speaking at the EPA hearing.

The stakes vary dramatically depending on where a project is sited. Georgia, for example, currently maintains a more robust public notification process for minor sources. Keri Powell, an Atlanta-based attorney at the Southern Environmental Law Center, noted that her group recently used information from public air permit applications to alert state utility officials to construction issues at a data center. Under the proposed rule, that kind of community intervention could become far less feasible.

"Georgia is an example of a place where I can say I'm concerned," Powell said.

Kentucky also maintains stronger public participation requirements for minor source permits, though Byron Gary, a senior attorney at the Kentucky Resources Council, cautioned that an informal state commitment to preserve those requirements could shift under a future administration. "Who knows [if] the next administration, whether they would actually change it," Gary said.

Texas presents a contrasting example. The state's data center boom has been accompanied by a large buildout of private gas plants operating under minor pollution permits, and lower enforcement levels have left some residents unaware of the scale of fossil fuel infrastructure being installed near their homes.

The timing of the proposed rule has not gone unnoticed by environmental advocates. Powell said the revision is "part of a package of rules that the Trump administration is pushing through to make it easier for AI data centers to be constructed" — a characterization that aligns with the administration's stated ambitions. The EPA spokesperson said the agency is working to make the United States "the AI capital of the world."

The proposal comes amid a broader federal push to remove barriers to data center development. Spending on data center construction outpaced spending on public transportation infrastructure for the first time in June, underscoring the scale of investment the industry has attracted.

With public opposition emerging as one of the remaining friction points in data center siting, the EPA's final decision on the rule will be closely watched by both the technology industry and the communities that stand in its path.
