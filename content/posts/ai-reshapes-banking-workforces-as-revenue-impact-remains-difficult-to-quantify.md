Banks across Europe and the United States are deploying artificial intelligence across operations, customer service, and compliance at an accelerating pace — but analysts say the technology's ability to generate measurable revenue gains remains elusive, even as headcount reductions mount.

This earnings season crystallized how differently institutions are framing the shift. UniCredit, Barclays, Santander, and ABN AMRO each referenced AI in their third-quarter results, while U.S. giants JPMorgan Chase and Goldman Sachs disclosed parallel changes to staffing and workflows.

Santander said AI initiatives generated 194 million euros in business value in the first nine months of 2026. Chief Financial Officer José García Cantera told CNBC's "Squawk Box Europe" on Oct. 28 that the technology is now embedded in "all the processes that we are conducting at the bank."

"AI basically helps, at least for now, decrease low value-added processes, and the company employees can then focus on more value-added processes — this affects both revenue and costs," Cantera said. "It's difficult to just to say how much comes from AI, but clearly AI is improving the quality of our services to clients, is helping us in asset quality, in cost management, and is having clearly a very positive impact."

UniCredit CEO Andrea Orcel described AI as "a lever that we didn't anticipate we would have, and working extremely well," in remarks to CNBC on Nov. 9. His bank — which reported its best-ever quarterly profit of 3.3 billion euros — is using the technology to accelerate a shift toward more commercial, higher-value client work.

ABN AMRO is deploying AI to support anti-money-laundering checks and expanding a partnership with Infosys to modernize its technology infrastructure. CEO Marguerite Bérard said in the bank's Nov. 11 third-quarter results that the improvements "make the bank easier to run and scale."

Headcount reductions are emerging as one of the clearest near-term signals. Barclays said it plans to cut 5,000 jobs — roughly 5% of its workforce — in 2026, with approximately 60% of those reductions affecting support functions, including developer roles. The bank said in its third-quarter earnings update that AI is not responsible for all the cuts.

Andrew Stimpson, managing director and head of European banks research at KBW, told CNBC that the cuts have "tended to come from back-office and support functions rather than the front office" and are "partly, but not solely, due to AI."

JPMorgan managers have been told to avoid hiring as the bank deploys AI across its businesses, CFO Jeremy Barnum told analysts during the lender's third-quarter earnings call. Goldman Sachs said it intends to "constrain headcount growth" and trim a limited number of positions.

A KBW study published in September found that customer service — including chatbots and routing customer requests — was the most frequently cited AI use case among European banks, followed by back-office functions such as operations, identity verification, and financial crime prevention. Just under half of the use cases tracked were aimed at cutting costs or improving efficiency, reinforcing that revenue generation remains a secondary goal.

Wealth management is drawing attention as the sector most likely to test whether AI can expand bank revenues rather than simply lower costs. Sophia Whitbread, an analyst at Ninety One, said the evidence is still strongest for AI improving service and employee productivity, rather than demonstrably generating revenue. She described wealth management as "the most important revenue test because AI can augment rather than replace the advisor."

"For European banks, the probable model is 'human adviser plus AI', especially at UBS, Standard Chartered and the private-banking units of Deutsche, ABN and the French banks, rather than a fully automated relationship," Whitbread said, pointing to JPMorgan's advisor and wealth-planning tools as a U.S. example of that approach.

On the hiring side, Mark Astbury, a director at recruitment consultant Morgan McKinley, said AI is reducing "traditional project management and back-office opportunities" while driving greater demand for "specialists in AI, software engineering and data."

Whether efficiency gains eventually translate into demonstrable revenue growth will likely define how investors and regulators assess the technology's true value to the sector in the year ahead.
