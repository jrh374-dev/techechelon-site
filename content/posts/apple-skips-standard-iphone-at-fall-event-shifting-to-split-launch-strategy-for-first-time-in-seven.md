Apple held its first major product showcase under new CEO John Ternus on Wednesday, unveiling the iPhone Duo — the company's first foldable phone — along with the iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max. Notably absent from the event was any standard, non-Pro iPhone model, marking the biggest change to Apple's fall release cadence in seven years.

The least expensive new iPhone announced at the event is the iPhone 18 Pro, which starts at $1,199 in the U.S. — a $100 increase over the entry price of the prior generation. The previous standard iPhone 17, which had been priced at $799, was relisted at $899 following the event.

Industry analysts say the omission of a standard iPhone 18 was widely anticipated. The expectation is that Apple will hold a separate spring event to launch its mainline, lower-priced model — a slot the company has previously used to announce devices such as the iPhone SE and iPhone 16e.

The split-launch approach appears designed in part to smooth out Apple's revenue across quarters. Historically, Apple's first fiscal quarter — the first full quarter of iPhone sales, which encompasses the holiday season — has been its strongest, with the following two quarters significantly slower.

"When Apple puts all the focus on the last quarter of the year, of course that generates quite strong revenues, but they were missing when the second quarter comes along with much lower sales," said Francisco Jeronimo, a vice president at market researcher IDC, in an interview. "I think this will give them some stability in terms of balancing the revenues throughout the year."

Bank of America analyst Wamsi Mohan acknowledged both the upside and the risk of the new strategy. "In our view, the split launch could support mix and ASPs," Mohan wrote in a note. He added, however, that it "creates some risk that price-sensitive consumers defer upgrades until the lower-priced models arrive."

Supply chain pressures appear to be a contributing factor as well. Apple raised prices on Macs and iPads in June, citing a global shortage in memory and other components — constraints that former CEO Tim Cook described as "unsustainable" in an interview with The Wall Street Journal. Releasing only two new iPhone models instead of four reduces the complexity of what Apple must secure from suppliers, according to Neil Shah, vice president of research at Counterpoint Research. "High memory pricing is not helping Apple launch multiple iPhones at the same time," Shah said.

In the first half of 2026, the iPhone 17 Pro and Pro Max together accounted for 54% of iPhone sales, while the standard iPhone 17 represented 26%, according to IDC estimates — underscoring the growing dominance of Apple's premium tier.

The headline product from Wednesday's event, the iPhone Duo, will start at $1,999. The foldable device supports multitasking across two apps simultaneously and is compatible with Apple's Pencil stylus. Craig Federighi, Apple's senior vice president of software engineering, said the company redesigned much of its iOS software to take advantage of the dual-screen form factor. "For a device that plays such a central role in your life, a larger display would open up entirely new possibilities," Ternus said at the launch. "We think the best way to achieve that is with a foldable design."

The iPhone Duo is powered by Apple's new C2 modem — the second in-house modem the company has produced — further reducing its reliance on Qualcomm. Johny Srouji, Apple's chief hardware officer, said in the keynote that the C2 is 50% faster than its predecessor and operates at 15% lower energy consumption.

Ternus, who replaced Tim Cook as CEO on September 1, is only the second Apple chief executive since Steve Jobs resigned in 2011. Wednesday's event served as his first major public showcase in the role, and the product decisions announced — higher price points, a deferred standard model, and a debut foldable — reflect a company navigating both supply constraints and a maturing smartphone market. The spring event, when and if it arrives, will be the clearer test of whether the split-launch gamble draws in the price-sensitive buyers Apple chose not to court this fall.
