Artificial intelligence is reshaping the hiring landscape at major financial institutions, with job postings for AI-related roles at banks including JPMorgan Chase, Citigroup, and Capital One climbing 49% this year compared with 2025 — reaching 139,819 listings, according to an analysis by enterprise hiring data firm Draup.

The fastest-growing cluster of skills centers on AI agents, with references to "agent orchestration" — the ability to design agents that work in concert on a single task — rising 1,721% this year, according to Draup, which draws its data from public job postings and platforms including LinkedIn.

"This is arguably the hottest skill on Wall Street," Draup CEO Vijay Swaminathan said. "It's a massive opportunity. They need people who understand data and people who understand AI and where to put it."

The surge reflects banks moving beyond chatbots and into what industry observers describe as the next phase of their AI strategy: deploying coordinated networks of specialized agents to handle increasingly complex workflows, from trading desk operations to compliance monitoring and back-office processing.

Deploying AI inside a financial institution often requires linking multiple specialized agents — one to inspect raw data, another to analyze a document, and a third to verify regulatory compliance, for example. The workers coordinating this process, frequently referred to as forward-deployed engineers, require a blend of technical proficiency and deep domain knowledge of specific business functions.

"There is a lot of complexity in an enterprise," Swaminathan said. "Sometimes these complexities are visible, but many times they are hidden. It takes a long time even to automate a simple process."

Other technical skills are rising alongside agent orchestration. References to LangGraph, a framework for building multistep workflows, jumped 679%, while mentions of LlamaIndex — which helps connect AI applications to data sources — rose 291%. References to retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, a technique for grounding AI models in company databases, climbed 259%, according to Draup.

The build-out is also generating substantial demand in governance and risk management. References to "responsible AI" in job postings surged 657% this year, while mentions of AI governance and risk management rose 394% and 359%, respectively. Governance-related skills now account for more than 16,000 references in Draup's data — nearly twice the roughly 8,400 tied to training, deploying, and running models directly.

"There is a lot of focus on making sure that the third parties that we are using in these products are not going rogue from a cybersecurity standpoint," Swaminathan said.

Soft skills are drawing renewed attention as well. Swaminathan said Draup's analysis shows growing emphasis on problem solving, creativity, and the ability to ask probing questions — attributes banks see as essential alongside technical competency.

The compensation premium for these roles is significant. Generative AI managers command a median base salary of approximately $190,000, according to Draup — above what comparable technology roles elsewhere in finance typically pay.

Despite the elevated pay, filling the positions remains difficult. Major banks are responding by leaning into internal reskilling programs, training existing developers and domain experts rather than relying solely on external hiring.

JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon has previously spoken of "huge redeployment plans" as AI absorbs more of the bank's routine labor — underscoring that the hiring wave and the displacement concern are advancing in parallel.

"I think the more we prioritize those soft skills with the right amount of technical skills, people will adapt and learn," Swaminathan said.

As banks commit deeper resources to building out agent infrastructure, the composition of their technology workforces is shifting — with governance, orchestration, and deployment expertise now rivaling traditional model-building roles in both volume and strategic importance.
