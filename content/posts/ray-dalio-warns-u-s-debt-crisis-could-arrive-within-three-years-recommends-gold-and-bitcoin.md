Bridgewater Associates founder Ray Dalio said Friday that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's debt buyback announcement fits into a broader pattern that could signal a forthcoming fiscal crisis, recommending investors shift portions of their portfolios into gold and bitcoin.

"I am confident that the government's financial condition is at an inflection point," Dalio wrote in a LinkedIn post published Friday. "If this is not dealt with now, the debts will build up to levels where they can't be managed without great trauma."

Bessent announced this week that the Treasury Department would at least double its buybacks of long-dated bonds beginning in September, with purchases expected to top $4 billion. Dalio acknowledged the move but noted the department has "only limited capacity" to buy back bonds — a constraint that, combined with Japan reducing its U.S. bond market exposure and surging long-dated American Treasury yields, he said warrants serious concern.

U.S. government debt crossed the $40 trillion mark this week for the first time, according to market data cited in financial reporting. The budget deficit topped $432 billion in July alone, with the U.S. currently spending roughly 40% more than it collects in revenue, Dalio said.

If the U.S. government were a private business, Dalio said debt service payments would amount to approximately $11 trillion — about 200% of annual revenue.

Dalio, 77, outlined a three-part strategy he said the U.S. must pursue simultaneously to bring the budget deficit down to 3% of gross domestic product: reduce government spending, increase tax revenue, and lower interest rates. "All three need to happen concurrently so as to prevent any one from being too large," he wrote. "If any one is too large, the adjustment will be traumatic."

He cautioned against forcing any single lever too hard. "It would be very bad if the Federal Reserve unnaturally forced interest rates down," he said, adding that changes should be implemented while the economy remains healthy, since a recession would require increased government outlays and narrow the window for adjustment.

On timing, Dalio acknowledged uncertainty. The exact onset of a potential crisis, he said, could be influenced by factors ranging from military conflict to political shifts. On its present course, he placed the range at one to five years. "My guess, which I suppose will be a bad one, is that it will come in three years, give or take two, if the course we're on is not changed."

For investors, Dalio recommended being underweight debt assets such as bonds. He suggested allocating as much as 10% to 15% of a portfolio to gold, as well as "a bit" of bitcoin.

Dalio's post arrived at the end of a volatile week for U.S. financial markets. The 30-year Treasury yield surged to multi-decade highs, pressuring equities. The S&P 500 snapped a three-week advance on Friday, while the Nasdaq Composite slid roughly 2% on the week. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF fell more than 4%, and the Magnificent Seven stocks as a group declined more than 1%.

The bond market turbulence sets up a consequential stretch for investors in the week ahead. The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge — the personal consumption expenditure price index for July — is due Wednesday, August 26, with economists polled by FactSet forecasting headline monthly inflation of 0.20% and a core annual rate of 3.2%. Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh is also scheduled to speak at the central bank's annual Jackson Hole symposium, where markets will be watching closely for any signal on the path of monetary policy. Nvidia's quarterly earnings, also due Wednesday, round out a week that analysts say could determine whether the S&P 500 has a clear path toward the 8,000 level.

Whether policymakers heed Dalio's call for a coordinated fiscal correction — or whether bond markets force the issue before Washington acts — remains the central question heading into the fall.
