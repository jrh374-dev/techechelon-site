BlackRock, Carhartt, Ford Motor Co., and Google announced Tuesday the formation of the Alliance for America's Skilled Trades, a joint initiative aimed at expanding access to workforce training as labor shortages in the skilled trades deepen across the United States.

The alliance intends to partner with labor unions and trade associations, invest in apprenticeship programs, and raise public awareness about career pathways into the trades, according to a joint statement released by the companies.

"Investment in America's infrastructure will help shape the country's long-term economic trajectory, but its success ultimately depends on the skilled workforce that brings these projects to life," Bayo Ogunlesi, BlackRock's chairman and chief executive officer of global infrastructure partners, said in a statement. "Expanding that talent pipeline requires long-term commitment and partnership across sectors."

The announcement comes as a shortage of skilled tradespeople — driven largely by experienced workers aging out of the workforce — has pushed up job openings and wages for electricians, heating, ventilation and air conditioning technicians, plumbers, and builders.

The labor market pressures extend beyond traditional trades. A February report by Citrini Research, an investment research firm, found that growing concern over AI displacing white-collar workers is creating a "negative feedback loop with no natural brake," contributing to a broader revaluation of vocational and technical training.

Enrollment data supports that shift. Certificate and associate degree programs each grew by approximately 2% in fall 2025, while bachelor's degree enrollments rose by less than 1%, according to the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center. A separate Lumina Foundation and Gallup poll found that Americans' confidence in four-year degrees is declining while two-year associate's degrees are gaining favor.

"Community colleges are doing really well with affordability and value," said Courtney Brown, chief data and research officer for the Lumina Foundation. "More people gravitate towards that."

The infrastructure dimension of the shortage is particularly acute. Paul Morgan, global chief operating officer of real estate management services at JLL, wrote in an April research note that the skilled trades shortfall "threatens how we power our data centers, cool our laboratories, secure our manufacturing facilities and maintain the spaces where millions of Americans work every day." Morgan described the situation as "a critical inflection point where demand for skilled trades is accelerating while the available workforce continues to shrink."

Google's participation underscores the tech industry's direct stake in the problem. The company is among the largest builders and operators of data centers, and continued infrastructure expansion is central to sustaining the broader AI build-out.

"Building the physical infrastructure for America's future requires significantly increasing the pipeline of skilled tradespeople across the country — a challenge that can only be addressed with collective action," Ruth Porat, president and chief investment officer of Alphabet and Google, said in a statement.

The alliance's cross-sector composition — spanning asset management, apparel, automotive, and technology — reflects the breadth of industries that depend on tradespeople to execute large-scale capital projects. Whether the initiative translates into measurable gains in workforce enrollment will likely depend on the scale and structure of the apprenticeship investments the companies commit to in the months ahead.
