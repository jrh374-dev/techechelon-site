The Trump administration has staked out a position that current criminal and civil laws are sufficient to hold artificial intelligence developers accountable, even as legal experts raise doubts about whether that framework can keep pace with rapidly evolving AI technology.

At the center of the strategy is the administration's so-called "Super Intelligence (SI) Force," which has argued it can establish standards for AI firms using laws already on the books — an approach that sidesteps the creation of new AI-specific legislation or a dedicated regulatory regime.

The stance represents a deliberate choice to avoid adding regulatory layers on top of an industry the administration has broadly sought to promote, reflecting a broader philosophy of limiting government intervention in emerging technology sectors.

Legal experts, however, are uncertain whether existing statutes were designed with AI agents in mind — particularly the newer generation of autonomous systems capable of taking actions with limited human oversight.

The core tension lies in the nature of AI agents themselves. Unlike traditional software, these systems can make consequential decisions across a wide range of contexts, from financial transactions to healthcare recommendations, raising questions about liability, intent, and accountability that existing tort and criminal frameworks may not cleanly resolve.

Critics of the administration's approach argue that current laws were written to govern human actors and well-understood technologies, and that applying them to AI systems introduces interpretive ambiguities that courts have not yet been asked to resolve at scale.

Supporters counter that a patchwork of new AI-specific rules could stifle development and create compliance burdens that benefit only the largest incumbents, leaving smaller developers at a disadvantage.

The administration's position comes as other major economies — most notably the European Union — have moved in the opposite direction, introducing dedicated AI legislation with tiered compliance requirements based on risk levels.

Whether the United States courts will be asked to test the limits of existing law against AI-driven harm in the near term remains an open question, but the administration's framework suggests those battles, if they come, will play out in existing judicial venues rather than before a new regulatory body.

For now, the SI Force's standards-setting role and the administration's reliance on current statutes will face their most meaningful test when — and if — a significant AI-related harm surfaces that demands clear legal accountability.
