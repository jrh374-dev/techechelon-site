A London neighborhood once defined by narcotics markets and street crime has become one of the most coveted addresses in artificial intelligence, drawing OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic, and dozens of smaller startups to a dense cluster of offices within walking distance of one another.

King's Cross, long known locally as the "Knowledge Quarter," now rivals San Francisco and Beijing as a global AI hub, according to TechCrunch, which profiled the neighborhood's rise in depth.

The transformation traces its origins to 2016, when DeepMind — by then newly acquired by Google — relocated to the area. Other AI companies followed, drawn by the concentration of talent and the proximity to Google DeepMind's operations. Today, names including Isomorphic Labs, Cusp AI, Wayne, Recursive, Synthesia, and Anthropic are all based in or near King's Cross, along with the recently relocated European Technology Network.

The scale of the shift is visible in London's broader real estate numbers. Since the start of June, AI-related startups have leased more than 1 million square feet of office space across London, according to the real estate firm Knight Frank. Prime rents in King's Cross have risen 18% over the past three years, driven largely by large leases of at least 10,000 square feet. The vacancy rate for conventional office space in the area has fallen to just 0.9%.

"Demand has outstripped supply," Chris Dunn, a commercial insight associate at Knight Frank, said.

London overall is home to around 3,600 AI startups, which together have raised approximately $12.1 billion of the $14.8 billion raised across the city since late July, according to data from Dealroom.

The talent competition accompanying the boom is acute. When Anthropic announced its move to London earlier this year, it listed a salary range of £260,000 to £630,000 for a machine learning research engineer — compared with an average London salary of around £102,000 for the same role, according to the article. Some founders say they are being forced to raise larger rounds simply to remain competitive in hiring.

A recurring theme among the area's investors and founders is sovereignty — the question of whether European companies can sustain themselves on infrastructure and AI capabilities built and controlled outside the region. That concern sharpened this summer when Anthropic shut off access to its Mythos and Fable tools, which some in the ecosystem took as a warning.

"Europe can't simply rent its AI capabilities and capacity; it needs to build and hold some of its own," Robin Klein, co-founder of the venture capital firm Phoenix Court, said. Phoenix Court is based in the King's Cross area and counts three portfolio companies in the vicinity.

Hussein Kanji, an investor at Hoxton Ventures, offered a blunter historical contrast. "In the '80s, crack and heroin made the area a major narcotics market," he said, recalling "syringes in tree trunks and gangs patrolling the streets." He noted that in 1982, the local church was occupied by the English Collective of Prostitutes for 12 consecutive days. "Now it is the AI hotbed of the United Kingdom. What a change."

Founders operating in the neighborhood say the momentum feels self-reinforcing. Simon Kohl, founder of Latent Labs, maintains offices in both King's Cross and San Francisco and said the London office is currently growing faster.

"The mood right now feels less like London trying to catch up and more like London becoming one of the default places to start a serious AI company," Kohl said.

Alex Kendall, who co-founded the autonomous vehicle company Wayve in 2017 and moved it to King's Cross in 2018, offered a similar assessment. "Ten years ago, building a frontier AI company from London felt like an unusual choice," he said. "Now it feels like an obvious one."

Whether the neighborhood's momentum translates into globally scaled companies — rather than outposts of U.S. labs — remains the central question for the ecosystem's longer-term ambitions.
