Spanning geopolitical conflict, trade policy upheaval, artificial intelligence, private market fragility, and bank regulation, the letter offers an unvarnished look at how the most influential banker in the world sees the current environment.

Dimon named geopolitical instability as the primary risk facing the bank, citing the wars in Ukraine and Iran, persistent Middle East hostilities, and growing tensions with China. 

He also addressed trade policy directly, noting that U.S. tariff battles "are clearly not over" and that many nations are now actively reassessing their trade arrangements in ways that will produce second- and third-order effects that remain difficult to model. 

While acknowledging the necessity of some reshoring for national security reasons, Dimon was candid about the difficulty of forecasting long-term outcomes: "The trade battles are clearly not over, and it should be expected that many nations are analyzing how and with whom they should create trade arrangements."

On artificial intelligence, Dimon reiterated his view that the investment cycle is not a speculative bubble, but acknowledged that predicting the ultimate winners and losers remains impossible at this stage. 

JPMorgan has deployed AI at scale across its business, and Dimon described agentic AI systems as central to the firm's competitive strategy going forward. 

At the same time, he warned that the technology's transformational impact carries second- and third-order societal effects that demand active monitoring — not merely optimization of known use cases. "We will not put our heads in the sand," he wrote. "We will deploy AI, as we deploy all technology, to do a better job for our customers and employees."

The letter's sharpest market commentary was directed at private credit. Dimon flagged a structural transparency problem: private credit funds generally do not mark their loan portfolios to market with the same rigor that public markets require, meaning investors may be underestimating the losses already embedded in these vehicles. 

"Actual losses are already higher than they should be relative to the environment," he said, predicting that insurance regulators will eventually insist on more rigorous ratings — a development he believes could trigger significant redemption pressure across the sector. Fears around loans made to software companies have already sparked steep redemption requests at several large private credit funds in recent weeks.

Dimon's annual letter has long been one of the most closely read documents in global finance — part performance review, part policy tract, part geopolitical commentary. This year's edition is particularly charged, arriving as JPMorgan prepares to report first-quarter earnings and as markets grapple with the volatility introduced by the U.S.-Iran war and elevated energy prices. 

For investors, the letter serves as a reminder that even the most stable of financial institutions views the current period as exceptional — and that the risks Dimon outlines are not abstract but actively shaping the bank's positioning today.
