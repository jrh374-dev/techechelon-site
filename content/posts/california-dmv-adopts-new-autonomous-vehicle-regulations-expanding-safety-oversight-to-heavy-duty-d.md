California's Department of Motor Vehicles adopted a new set of autonomous vehicle regulations on Tuesday, extending safety and oversight requirements to heavy-duty driverless vehicles and opening the door for their testing and deployment on public roads.

The updated rules expand the state's existing AV regulatory framework to cover all types of autonomous vehicles, including larger commercial platforms that had previously operated in a more ambiguous regulatory space, the DMV said in a statement.

Among the most significant provisions is a new mechanism allowing law enforcement agencies to cite AV operators for violations—a step regulators say is intended to strengthen accountability for manufacturers and fleet operators alike.

The DMV described the changes as part of a broader effort to curb public safety concerns as autonomous vehicles become more prevalent on California streets and highways.

California has long served as the primary regulatory proving ground for autonomous vehicle technology in the United States, hosting testing programs from companies including Waymo, Cruise, and several commercial trucking-focused developers. The new rules extend that oversight to the heavy-duty segment, which has seen growing interest from freight and logistics operators.

The regulations come amid heightened public and legislative scrutiny of AV deployments. A series of high-profile incidents involving robotaxi and autonomous freight vehicles in California and other states over the past several years contributed to calls for more rigorous oversight frameworks.

By incorporating heavy-duty vehicles—a category that includes large trucks and commercial transport platforms—into the same safety and oversight regime as passenger AVs, the DMV is signaling that regulators intend to treat the two segments with equal scrutiny as the technology matures.

The updated rules also reflect a tension the industry has navigated for years: manufacturers want permissive testing environments to iterate on their technology, while regulators and local governments have pushed for enforceable standards that can be applied when things go wrong.

With the new framework now adopted, manufacturers seeking to test or deploy heavy-duty autonomous vehicles in California will be subject to the expanded requirements, and law enforcement will have a clearer basis to act on observed violations—a combination that could shape how quickly companies move to scale operations in the state.
