Paris Hilton and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) stood on the steps of the U.S. Capitol on Tuesday to urge the House of Representatives to bring the Defiance Act to a floor vote, two years after versions of the bill first stalled in the chamber.

The Defiance Act — formally the Disrupt Explicit Forged Images And Non-Consensual Edits Act — would expand on existing federal law by giving victims of sexually explicit AI-generated deepfakes a civil right to sue those who knowingly create, share, publish, solicit, or threaten to distribute such content without consent. Victims could also recover legal costs and seek court orders requiring removal of the material.

The U.S. Senate passed the most recent version of the bipartisan bill by unanimous consent in January 2026. The House has yet to hold a vote on any version of it.

Hilton is currently serving as chief advocacy officer at Loti AI, a digital identity protection firm that helps people locate unauthorized uses of their likeness or voice online and file takedown requests. She said the company has detected more than 100,000 explicit deepfakes depicting her and helped remove more than 60,000 of them.

"I'm very fortunate that I have a team of lawyers. I have people helping me. But a lot of these young girls and women don't have that support," Hilton told CNBC's Julia Boorstin during an episode of the "CNBC Changemakers" podcast.

Hilton also described the broader human toll of the technology, noting that some teenagers have died by suicide after explicit synthetic images of them circulated among classmates. "Even though the image is not real, the pain and suffering that comes along with it is very real," she said.

The advocacy push comes despite the existence of a related federal law. The Take It Down Act, signed by President Trump in May 2025, made certain disclosures of nonconsensual intimate images — including AI-generated deepfakes — a federal crime, and required covered platforms to remove flagged material and known identical copies within 48 hours of receiving a valid request.

Hilton and other critics argue that law has gaps. Because it relies on federal prosecutors rather than providing a separate civil remedy, victims who cannot prompt government action have limited recourse. Hilton said current federal law sometimes "doesn't work," leaving survivors without a viable path to hold perpetrators accountable.

The Sexual Violence Prevention Association, which helped draft the Defiance Act, delivered letters to House Speaker Mike Johnson and House Majority Leader Steve Scalise on Tuesday calling for the bill to be brought to the floor.

Joining Hilton and Ocasio-Cortez at Tuesday's Capitol appearance was Rep. Laurel Lee (R-FL), reflecting the bill's bipartisan backing.

Hilton's personal history with nonconsensual image distribution predates the AI era. At 19, she was pressured into allowing an intimate recording with her then-boyfriend, Rick Salomon, which she expected to remain private before it was released without her consent. She has described AI as creating a scalable amplification of that same harm.

"Somebody can literally just take an innocent photo and turn it into some explicit photo or video, and this is happening to millions of women and children all around the world, and they don't have anyone to protect them because the laws have just not caught up with this technology yet," Hilton said.

Whether House leadership schedules a vote remains the central question, as advocates continue building pressure ahead of what could be another legislative session without action on the measure.
