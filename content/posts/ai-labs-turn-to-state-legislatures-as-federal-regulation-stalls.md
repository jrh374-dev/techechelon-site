Major artificial intelligence companies are no longer waiting for Washington to establish a national regulatory framework, instead shifting their attention to state capitals where they are actively shaping legislation to define their own policy boundaries, according to reporting on the matter.

The pivot comes as Congress has struggled to advance a unified federal standard for AI, leaving a regulatory vacuum that states have moved to fill with their own patchwork of rules.

Most leading AI laboratories say they would prefer a single national safety framework — one that would supersede the growing tangle of state-level statutes — but in the absence of congressional action, they are engaging directly with state lawmakers to ensure any emerging rules reflect their priorities.

The strategy reflects a tension at the heart of the AI policy debate: companies that publicly champion federal uniformity are simultaneously working to influence the very state-by-state fragmentation they say they want to avoid.

The White House has not advanced a comprehensive AI regulatory proposal, and Congress has yet to move major standalone legislation, leaving the industry in a prolonged period of regulatory ambiguity.

State governments, meanwhile, have filled the void. Legislatures across the country have introduced or passed measures covering areas ranging from algorithmic accountability and disclosure requirements to liability standards for AI-generated content — each with its own scope, definitions, and enforcement mechanisms.

For AI developers operating at national and international scale, navigating dozens of distinct legal regimes poses both operational and legal complexity, analysts say, which is part of why the companies are choosing to engage rather than wait.

The dynamic mirrors patterns seen in earlier technology policy debates — most notably around data privacy, where years of federal inaction produced a fragmented landscape of state laws that industry groups subsequently lobbied to preempt with a national standard.

Critics of the industry's state-level engagement argue that AI labs are using their lobbying influence to water down or redirect state legislation in ways that would benefit their commercial interests, even as they call publicly for robust safety standards.

Whether Washington ultimately advances a federal framework remains an open question, and the outcome will likely depend in part on how the current state-level activity unfolds — and whether the resulting regulatory divergence creates enough pressure on Congress to act.
