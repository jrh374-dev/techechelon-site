The Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously on Wednesday to keep the benchmark federal funds rate in a range of 4.25 to 4.50 percent, marking the third consecutive pause and the seventh meeting without a cut.

Chair Jerome Powell, speaking to reporters after the decision, framed the hold as a function of "two competing signals that have not yet resolved." Inflation, he said, remained sticky in services. Labor data, by contrast, was showing "the first clear signs of softening since last fall."

The committee's official statement made the shift explicit. The reference to a "strong" labor market — present in every statement since November — was downgraded to "moderating." Bond markets reacted within seconds: the two-year yield fell six basis points, and traders trimmed the implied odds of a June cut from 47 percent to 31 percent.

## What the doves wanted

Two voting members had reportedly pressed for a quarter-point cut in the weeks leading up to the meeting, citing the latest payrolls revision and the rise in continuing claims. They were overruled, but the language shift is widely read as a concession to that argument.

## What changes in June

A cut is now genuinely back in play. Three more inflation prints arrive before the June meeting, and the committee's tolerance for sticky services inflation has clearly narrowed. The path to a cut runs through the May CPI release on June 11. If services inflation comes in flat or lower, the case for action becomes hard to deny.
