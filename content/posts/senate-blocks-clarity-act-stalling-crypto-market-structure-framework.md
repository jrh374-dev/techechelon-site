The U.S. Senate on Tuesday voted to block the Clarity Act from advancing, delivering a significant blow to the cryptocurrency industry's effort to establish a comprehensive regulatory framework and leaving the legislation stalled on Capitol Hill.

The procedural cloture vote attracted 50 votes in favor and 49 against — well short of the 60 votes required to clear the procedural hurdle and advance the bill to full Senate debate.

The outcome came despite months of negotiations aimed at assembling bipartisan support. Republican leaders had released a revised version of the bill on Sunday, incorporating new ethics restrictions designed to address Democratic concerns about public officials profiting from crypto ventures. Those changes ultimately failed to bring enough Democrats on board.

Sen. Ruben Gallego, D-Ariz., a key Democratic negotiator on the bill, said before the vote that insufficient Republican concessions on ethics language were to blame. "The compromise we had was a good ethics compromise that would have bought a lot of Dem votes," Gallego said. He charged that Republicans cared "more about making sure the president keeps making money than actually bringing regulations," and were consequently "failing the whole system."

Democratic frustration centered specifically on profits gained from crypto ventures by President Donald Trump and his family, an issue negotiators were unable to resolve before Tuesday's vote.

Sen. Cynthia Lummis, R-Wyo., the bill's most prominent Senate champion, told reporters earlier Tuesday that "it's over" if the procedural vote failed.

Markets reacted swiftly. Bitcoin fell 3%, while Coinbase shares dropped 8% and Circle shares slid 10% amid the broader sell-off that followed the vote.

The Clarity Act would have established a framework for digital assets, divided oversight responsibilities between the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, set registration requirements for crypto firms, and strengthened protections against money laundering.

Even a successful cloture vote would not have guaranteed passage. The bill would still have needed to survive additional Senate negotiations and votes, clear the House, and reach the president's desk.

The timeline for a revival looks difficult. Senators are scheduled to leave Washington in early October and are not expected to return until after the midterm election — now seven weeks away. The House recesses even earlier, heading out at the end of this week. The failed vote likely means the industry will have to wait until next year for legislative clarity, according to the bill's supporters.

Much of the industry had already begun to hedge its expectations, looking to regulatory agencies for incremental progress in the absence of legislation. The SEC has proposed allowing startups to sell as much as $75 million of tokens without registering, while the CFTC recently approved the first bitcoin perpetual futures in the United States.

Tuesday's result may also activate Fairshake, a crypto political action committee, which could direct donations toward candidates running against senators who voted to block the bill's advancement — underscoring how deeply the industry intends to make the vote a political liability heading into the midterms.
