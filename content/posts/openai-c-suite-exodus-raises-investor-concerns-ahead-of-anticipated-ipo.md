OpenAI is heading into its anticipated public offering with a thinning executive bench, after revenue chief Denise Dresser announced her departure Thursday — just two days after operating chief Brad Lightcap said he was leaving after an eight-year tenure at the ChatGPT maker.

The back-to-back exits leave OpenAI's C-suite notably diminished at a critical moment. Dresser, Lightcap, and Fidji Simo — who left in July citing a "severe exacerbation of a chronic illness" — are now all gone, joining four other executives who departed in April, including Kevin Weil, the vice president of OpenAI for Science, and marketing chief Kate Rouch.

"The executives leaving OpenAI ahead of their IPO is a huge red flag," Kevin McCormick, founder of AI startup SignAudit.AI, wrote in a post on X on Thursday. McCormick added that Dresser likely walked away from a large pay package by leaving after less than a year. "If the executives leaving aren't being 'made whole' by the next company, it's bad news for OpenAI," he wrote.

OpenAI confidentially filed its IPO prospectus in June but has not provided a timeline for a future offering. The company carries a $852 billion valuation, which it must defend against growing investor concerns about competition from Google and Anthropic, increased adoption of lower-cost open-weight models, and comparisons to SpaceX's volatile stock price in its first two months on the market.

To fill Dresser's role, OpenAI named Dali Rajic — former chief operating officer of cybersecurity company Wiz, which Google acquired for $32 billion earlier this year — as its new chief revenue officer. Rajic was introduced to OpenAI through Thrive founder Josh Kushner, according to a person with knowledge of the matter. Thrive has been among OpenAI's most prominent backers.

President Greg Brockman, in a statement Thursday, said Dresser had led the revenue group through a "formative period for the business," and that Rajic "will turn what we've learned into repeatable execution as we build out the full system to make AI broadly useful for people and businesses."

Brockman also told employees in an internal Slack message that run rate revenue increased more than 20% month over month in July, including 32% growth for business customers. Under Dresser's leadership, the enterprise business grew to 2 million customers, doubling from a year prior.

OpenAI's financial backers were surprised by Dresser's announcement, according to two current investors who asked not to be named. They said that rapid turnover is consistent with the company's fast-moving culture, describing the environment as a pressure cooker — a characterization echoed by two former employees who also requested anonymity.

Dresser had joined OpenAI in December from Slack, where she had built a reputation in enterprise software following more than a decade in senior roles at Salesforce. In April, she told CNBC that enterprise made up 40% of the company's revenue and was "on track to reach parity with consumer by the end of 2026."

CFO Sarah Friar is set to meet with investors on Friday in a session that had been scheduled in advance, but she is expected to address the C-suite shakeup. Brockman will also attend to discuss company strategy, according to a person familiar with the plans who was not authorized to speak publicly.

The leadership turbulence arrives as AI competitors are also making moves. Anthropic CFO Krishna Rao is conducting early meetings with potential investors ahead of a possible IPO, though those discussions have remained high level and have not included specific financials or a valuation, sources said. Separately, Databricks closed a $5 billion funding round at a $190 billion valuation this week.

Whether the executive departures represent a structural problem or a natural byproduct of OpenAI's rapid growth will be a central question for prospective IPO investors — and CFO Friar's Friday meeting may offer the first meaningful opportunity to shape that narrative.
