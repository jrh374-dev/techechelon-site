Key artificial intelligence industry leaders signed a voluntary self-policing agreement at the White House on Tuesday, as President Trump simultaneously announced that the U.S. government would officially rebrand AI under the term "superintelligence."

The agreement, formally titled "The White House Accord on Superintelligence Joint Commitment on Frontier SI Responsibilities," was announced by Trump and Speaker Mike Johnson (R-La.) following a meeting with AI executives at the White House.

Johnson described the accord as laying out a series of voluntary commitments for AI developers, underscoring the self-regulatory nature of the arrangement. Trump characterized the document as "morally binding," though it carries no legal enforcement mechanism.

The White House meeting came as Trump has spent recent weeks pushing to retire the term "artificial intelligence" in favor of "superintelligence." He declared at the United Nations that official U.S. documents would use the new terminology going forward.

"It is the biggest thing there is," Trump said of the technology, reflecting the administration's framing of AI development as a matter of national priority.

The renaming effort signals a rhetorical shift intended to elevate the perceived stakes of the technology, even as critics and industry observers have noted that the term "superintelligence" carries a specific technical meaning in AI research — one that does not describe current systems.

The accord itself adds to a growing body of voluntary AI safety commitments that the White House has sought from industry since Trump returned to office, reinforcing an approach that favors industry self-governance over statutory regulation.

Whether the pledge produces measurable changes in developer conduct remains an open question, as voluntary accords in the technology sector have historically faced scrutiny over accountability and follow-through.

Separately, Congress is weighing legislation that would affect the infrastructure underlying AI development. Sen. Jon Husted's (R-Ohio) Ratepayer Protection Act, which cleared the House by a 417-3 vote, aims to address electricity costs associated with AI data centers by requiring states and some electric utilities to consider a standard under which data centers would bear a greater share of those costs.

Even if the bill passes, analysts have noted it is unclear whether it would have a meaningful impact on ratepayers, highlighting the gap between legislative intent and practical effect in a sector where energy demand continues to grow.

Together, the White House accord and the Ratepayer Protection Act reflect the breadth of policy activity now surrounding AI — spanning voluntary ethical commitments at the executive level and infrastructure cost questions in Congress — as the administration works to shape the governance landscape before more formal regulatory frameworks emerge.
