Microsoft's Xbox division will end unlimited cloud gaming access for Game Pass subscribers in November, replacing it with monthly hour caps tied to each subscription tier, the company announced Thursday — a shift driven by the rising cost of delivering streaming games at scale.

Game Pass Ultimate subscribers, who pay $22.99 per month, will receive 15 hours of cloud gaming. Game Pass Premium holders, at $14.99 per month, will have 10 hours, and Game Pass Essential subscribers, at $9.99 per month, will receive five hours, according to an Xbox blog post.

Players who exceed their monthly allotment will be able to purchase additional hours, though the company did not specify pricing for those add-ons. Non-subscribers will also gain the ability to buy cloud gaming time to stream titles they already own.

"We're introducing this model because the cost of providing cloud gaming grows as more people use it and play for longer," Xbox said in the blog post. "Moving to monthly limits allows us to keep offering the service while continuing to invest in its reliability and performance. We understand that for some players the practical result is a higher cost."

Microsoft said only 4% of Game Pass subscribers currently spend more than the new monthly limits streaming games through the cloud — suggesting the caps are designed to contain costs from a relatively small but resource-intensive cohort of heavy users.

The move reflects a broader cost-management push across Microsoft's product divisions. The company has increasingly shifted customers of services like GitHub Copilot and Microsoft 365 Copilot onto usage-based billing models as demand for computing resources climbs.

Inside Xbox, new chief executive Asha Sharma has been evaluating business model changes aimed at returning the division to growth and improving profitability. In July, Xbox said it would test an ad-supported game streaming option — another signal that the unit is exploring multiple paths to better unit economics.

The November cap announcement comes as Microsoft, like its cloud-computing peers, confronts finite infrastructure capacity against expanding consumer demand. Metered access, long standard in enterprise software, is now migrating into consumer entertainment products as providers seek tighter alignment between revenue and resource consumption.

For subscribers who stay within their monthly allotment, the day-to-day experience of Game Pass will remain unchanged. The pressure falls on the minority of high-volume users, who will face a choice between moderating their streaming habits or paying more — a dynamic that mirrors the usage-based structures already embedded in Microsoft's AI and productivity offerings.
