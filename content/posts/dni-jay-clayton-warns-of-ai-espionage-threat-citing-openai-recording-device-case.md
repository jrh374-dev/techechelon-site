Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton told CNBC on Friday that both U.S. companies and the Trump administration "need to be wary" of foreign espionage attempts targeting artificial intelligence, citing a recently surfaced federal case as evidence of the lengths adversaries will go to close the gap with American AI leadership.

Clayton, who was appointed to lead a new White House AI task force earlier this month, made the remarks during an appearance on "Squawk Box," where he was asked how concerned Americans should be in light of the case involving Saudi national Abdulrahman Alfozan, a former OpenAI employee charged with allegedly installing a recording device in an OpenAI executive conference room.

Alfozan has not been charged with espionage; the federal charge involves alleged interception of oral communications. Federal prosecutors noted that prior to his arrest, Alfozan had booked a one-way plane ticket to Saudi Arabia with his wife and another family member.

"The short answer to your question is espionage, both commercial espionage, and I would say state-sponsored espionage, are realities," Clayton told CNBC. "It's something that our private sector companies need to be wary of. We, as a government, need to be wary of."

Clayton said the OpenAI incident "shows the lengths that people will go to try and supplant our leadership in this, or at least stay close to us in this emerging technology." An ex-CIA official, speaking on condition of anonymity to discuss non-public information, told CNBC this week that the case is "probably the tip of the iceberg."

Beyond the specifics of the OpenAI case, Clayton framed AI as a force that "amplifies" existing cybersecurity risks across U.S. digital infrastructure — systems he described as interconnected with critical public goods, national defense, and global communications. That interconnectedness is "why we're focused on hardening those systems," he said.

Clayton identified China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea as nations with "significant technical capability" that the United States must stay ahead of. "We're not the only ones developing it. There are vectors in this technology that are coming from some of our known adversaries and competitors," he said.

The DNI also detailed progress from the task force's inaugural meeting at the White House on Thursday. He said the session built on an accord signed last month by President Donald Trump and a number of AI industry leaders — an agreement Trump described at the time as "morally binding" and which includes industry pledges to police AI risks independently.

The task force established three subcommittees at that first meeting. One is focused on reporting and response, a second on assessing critical infrastructure and coordinating with the private sector, and a third on law enforcement and responsibility, Clayton said.

The remarks underscore a broader shift in how the administration is framing AI competition — less as a purely economic or technological contest and more as a national security challenge requiring active counterintelligence measures alongside the development and deployment of the technology itself.

How quickly the task force's subcommittees move from structure to action — and whether the Alfozan case prompts stricter security requirements for AI labs working on sensitive research — will be closely watched in the months ahead.
