Former Rep. Mike Rogers, the Republican Senate nominee in Michigan, on Thursday called for a one-year pause on new data center construction, adding his voice to a growing chorus of politicians treating the infrastructure backbone of the artificial intelligence industry as a liability rather than an asset.

Rogers issued the call in a statement, framing the proposed moratorium as a response to concerns about the rapid proliferation of server facilities across the country — facilities that have drawn complaints in many communities over water usage, energy consumption, noise, and strain on local power grids.

The move reflects what observers describe as increasing political toxicity surrounding data centers, even as the technology industry continues to press for expanded infrastructure to support AI workloads.

Data centers have become a flashpoint in a number of local and state-level political contests, with residents in rural and suburban areas pushing back against large-scale facilities that can draw significant municipal resources while employing relatively few workers.

Rogers is competing in what analysts consider a critical Senate race in Michigan, a state that has seen its share of debates over industrial land use and energy policy. The moratorium proposal positions him alongside a faction of politicians — spanning both parties — who have grown skeptical of Big Tech's infrastructure ambitions at the local level.

The AI boom has accelerated demand for data center capacity, with major cloud providers and AI developers announcing multi-billion-dollar construction programs across dozens of states over the past several years. That expansion has outpaced the ability of some regional power grids to absorb new load, prompting pushback from utility regulators and elected officials alike.

Rogers did not specify in his statement which types of facilities the moratorium would cover, whether it would apply federally or only within Michigan, or what conditions would need to be met before construction could resume after the proposed one-year pause.

The political environment around data center siting is likely to remain volatile heading into November, as candidates in competitive races seek to distance themselves from constituencies that have grown frustrated with large technology projects arriving in their communities with limited local input.
