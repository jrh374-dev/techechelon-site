Flock Safety, the AI-powered license plate reader network company, announced changes to its privacy and data retention policies Thursday amid a growing wave of lawsuits and terminated contracts from communities and civil liberties advocates concerned about mass surveillance.

The Atlanta-based company has faced intensifying scrutiny across the country as privacy advocates and local governments question whether its network of automated license plate readers poses risks of misuse and unchecked surveillance.

The policy changes were announced in an apparent effort to stem the backlash, which has resulted in some municipalities ending their agreements with the company.

Flock's readers are used by law enforcement agencies nationwide to capture and log license plate data as vehicles pass detection points. Critics argue the technology enables the tracking of individuals' movements at scale, raising concerns about civil liberties implications that the company's existing policies did not adequately address.

Privacy advocates and civil liberties organizations have pushed back against the expansion of such networks, arguing that the breadth of data collection — and the potential for that data to be shared across agencies — creates surveillance infrastructure that outpaces current legal frameworks designed to protect citizens.

The company's announcement did not immediately resolve the concerns of all critics. Privacy lawsuits against Flock remain active in multiple jurisdictions, and the extent to which the revised policies satisfy objections from advocacy groups and local officials remains to be seen.

The episode reflects a broader tension playing out in cities and counties across the United States, where law enforcement agencies have adopted AI-assisted surveillance tools faster than privacy regulations have evolved to govern them.

Flock has marketed its platform as a tool for solving property crimes and locating stolen vehicles, and the company has expanded its footprint rapidly in recent years by partnering with both police departments and private homeowners' associations.

Whether the policy revisions will be sufficient to halt further contract cancellations — or slow the pace of litigation — is likely to depend on how clearly the company can demonstrate that data collected by its readers is subject to meaningful limitations on access, retention, and interagency sharing.
