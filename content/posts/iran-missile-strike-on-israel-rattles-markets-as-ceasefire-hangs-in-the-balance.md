Iran launched ballistic missiles at northern Israel on Sunday, threatening a fragile ceasefire with the United States and sending stock futures lower as investors absorbed a new layer of geopolitical risk atop an already turbulent week.

Futures tied to the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 150 points at the open Sunday night before paring some losses, with S&P 500 futures and Nasdaq 100 futures each shedding 0.2% in later trading. The moves came after President Trump was briefed on the incident, according to a U.S. official.

The Israeli Defense Forces confirmed the strike on the social platform X. Iranian Parliament Speaker MB Ghalibaf had posted on X arguing that a U.S. naval blockade and alleged breaches of agreements related to Lebanon constituted violations of the ceasefire — framing the missile launch as a response to those perceived infractions.

The attack landed at a delicate moment for financial markets. On Friday, the Nasdaq Composite dropped 4.18% to 25,709.43 — its steepest single-day decline since April 2025. The S&P 500 fell 2.64% to close at 7,383.74, and the Dow lost 695 points to finish at 50,866.78, one day after hitting a record high.

For the week, the S&P 500 dropped more than 2%, the Nasdaq fell 4.7%, and the Dow edged lower.

Friday's slide was already set in motion by a stronger-than-expected May jobs report, which pushed Treasury yields higher and amplified concerns that elevated financing costs could weigh on companies investing heavily in AI infrastructure.

"The stock market may be becoming a victim of its own success," said Callie Cox, chief market strategist at Ritholtz Wealth Management. "The job market has turned around, yet the threat of persistently high inflation seems to be the risk looming on everyone's minds."

Cox added that momentum-driven strategies had outpaced most of the market since the March lows — a dynamic she described as inconsistent with a high-rate, high-inflation environment and therefore potentially vulnerable. "Growth and momentum have outpaced almost everything since the March lows," she said. "That's not what you'd expect in a high-rate, high-inflation environment, and these strategies may be vulnerable to disappointment if cost pressures stay elevated."

Investors face a dense calendar in the week ahead. The May Consumer Price Index is due Wednesday, followed by the Producer Price Index on Thursday — both expected to reflect ongoing inflationary pressure.

Also on Friday, Elon Musk's SpaceX is set to make its public market debut, an offering widely expected to rank among the largest in Wall Street history. Cox described the timing as charged, noting that major IPOs have historically coincided with peak market exuberance. "Blockbuster offerings have marked the peak of excess in past market cycles, so there seems to be an awkward silence around what this could signal for sentiment," she said. "Many investors seem restrained and skeptical, but can that temperament exist when the biggest IPO of all time is on deck?"

The confluence of geopolitical risk, inflation anxiety, and a high-profile equity offering sets up a pivotal week for markets already contending with questions about whether AI-driven valuations can withstand a higher-rate environment — and now, whether a renewed conflict in the Middle East will add fresh volatility to an unsettled backdrop.
