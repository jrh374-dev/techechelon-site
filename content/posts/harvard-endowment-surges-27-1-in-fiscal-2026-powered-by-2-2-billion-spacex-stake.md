Harvard University's endowment delivered a 27.1% return in fiscal year 2026 — its strongest performance in five years — driven in large part by a $2.2 billion position in SpaceX that the school disclosed earlier this year, according to the university's annual financial report released Thursday.

The endowment, managed by the Harvard Management Company, now totals nearly $68 billion. The return exceeded the S&P 500's 22.3% total return over the same period by 4.8 percentage points.

SpaceX, Elon Musk's rocket and satellite company, was the endowment's single largest holding by value. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company came in second at just under $350 million, according to Harvard's second-quarter 13F filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

Harvard's fiscal year ends June 30. The 2026 return marks the endowment's best since 2021.

Harvard's chief financial officer Ritu Kalra and treasurer Timothy Barakett noted the gains in the school's 49-page annual report, writing that "while markets can easily take back what they give, that return will strengthen the endowment's capacity to support the University over time." They added that "the University deliberately smooths distributions so that operating support does not rise and fall with the markets."

The strong investment results arrive against a complicated financial backdrop. The Trump administration has sought to penalize the school and withhold funding over diversity commitments and other policy disputes. Federally-sponsored research revenue fell 19.5% to $509 million in fiscal 2026 as those pressures mounted.

Looking ahead, Kalra and Barakett flagged a steep increase in the federal excise tax on Harvard's net investment income, which is set to rise nearly six-fold — from 1.4% to 8% — beginning in the current fiscal year. "We estimate the higher rate will cost roughly $300 million a year," they wrote, noting that in a year with returns like fiscal 2026, the tax burden "would be several times larger."

University President Alan Garber acknowledged the headwinds but expressed confidence in Harvard's trajectory. "Together, we have the opportunity to set the University on a path to excellence that none of us can imagine today," he said in the report. "Notwithstanding the challenges and uncertainty, this is a thrilling time to be part of a great American institution devoted to Veritas."

The endowment's outperformance reflects a broader shift among elite university investment offices toward private market holdings — including late-stage private companies like SpaceX — that are not available to most retail investors. Harvard's willingness to concentrate a $2.2 billion position in a single private-market name underscores how aggressively institutional allocators have leaned into that strategy.

Whether fiscal 2027 can sustain the momentum remains an open question. The excise tax increase and the ongoing loss of federal research funding represent structural drains on Harvard's financial position that investment returns alone may not fully offset.
