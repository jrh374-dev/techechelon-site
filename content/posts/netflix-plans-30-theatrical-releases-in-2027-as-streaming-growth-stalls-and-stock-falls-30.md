Netflix is leaning into theatrical distribution even as it insists its identity remains rooted in streaming, a tension that has grown sharper following a quarter marked by viewership concerns, a significant leadership overhaul, and a stock decline of roughly 30% since January.

During the company's third-quarter earnings call on Tuesday, co-CEO Ted Sarandos reaffirmed that content — on which Netflix spent $20 billion last year — is designed first for subscribers on the streaming platform. At the same time, the company confirmed it is set to bring 30 films to cinemas in 2027, on par with its 2026 theatrical slate.

Not all of those releases are expected to have wide runs or extended theatrical windows. But Netflix is testing a more expansive model with at least one high-profile title: director Greta Gerwig's "Narnia: The Magician's Nephew," which will receive a wide release and a 49-day exclusive theatrical window when it arrives in cinemas in February.

The move comes as Netflix grapples with what Sarandos described as execution shortfalls in the United States. "Our business and our business flywheel are working," Sarandos told investors. "There is no need for a fundamental shift in the strategy. We do need better execution in the U.S. ... It's just not growing as fast as it could or as fast as we'd like."

Sarandos said the streamer had "some really solid doubles and triples this year," but suggested it needs more "home runs" — a reference to the kind of tentpole hits that previously drove subscriber growth, including "Stranger Things," "Bridgerton," and "Squid Game."

Analyst Alicia Reese of Wedbush wrote in a note Wednesday that theatrical exposure could serve Netflix's broader interests. "Netflix's more amenable attitude toward exclusive theatrical windows will not only facilitate better relationships with talent, but also add a new revenue stream and build interest in its streaming service," Reese wrote. "Theatrical releases don't cannibalize the streaming window, as Netflix fears."

Matthew Condon, equity research analyst at Citizens, told CNBC that theatrical could help Netflix build the kind of durable intellectual property it has historically struggled to cultivate. "If they really want to build stickier fan bases around some of their [intellectual property], I think theatrical could be a good avenue for them to do that," Condon said.

Ahead of the earnings report, Netflix executed a sweeping restructuring of its content and film leadership. The company brought in former Disney executive Peter Rice as chief content officer, replacing 10-year veteran Bela Bajaria. It also dismissed Dan Lin, its head of film, according to a person familiar with the matter who spoke on condition of anonymity to discuss internal changes.

Those moves followed a broader workforce reduction of roughly 1,000 employees, which included Lin's deputy Doug Belgrad and Sheroum Kim, the director of independent film. A successor to Lin has not been named.

The leadership shakeup reflects pressure on Netflix to reassert creative quality and shore up talent relationships in an increasingly competitive market. Sarandos acknowledged earlier this month that Netflix has lost filmmakers because the company would not commit to wide theatrical releases. Among the departures, "Stranger Things" creators Matt and Ross Duffer signed a four-year exclusive deal with Skydance.

Netflix has collaborated with prominent directors including Rian Johnson, Martin Scorsese, Alfonso Cuarón, Bong Joon Ho, Spike Lee, and Guillermo del Toro — though none of their Netflix projects received wide theatrical distribution or extended cinema runs.

The company also flagged in its shareholder letter that it is aware of investor concern about view-hours growth, describing engagement as "healthy" while stopping short of dismissing the worry. Netflix's foiled attempt to acquire the Warner Bros. film studio has kept attention on what role, if any, theatrical ownership might eventually play in its strategy.

Whether a 49-day window for a single Gerwig film evolves into a systematic theatrical commitment — or remains an occasional experiment — will likely depend on how "Narnia" and similar releases perform heading into 2027.
