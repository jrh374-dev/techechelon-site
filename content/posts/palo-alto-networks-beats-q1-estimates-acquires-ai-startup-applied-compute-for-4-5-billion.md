Palo Alto Networks reported fiscal first-quarter results that topped Wall Street expectations on Wednesday and announced a $4.5 billion all-stock acquisition of AI startup Applied Compute, sending shares up more than 7% in after-hours trading.

The cybersecurity company posted adjusted earnings per share of $1.05, surpassing analyst estimates of 97 cents, while revenue came in at $3.44 billion against a consensus expectation of $3.31 billion, according to LSEG estimates.

Revenue climbed 39% from $2.47 billion in the year-ago quarter. Net income rose to $482 million, or 57 cents per share, from $334 million, or 47 cents per share, a year earlier.

Annual recurring revenue for the company's next-generation security business jumped 66% year over year to $9.73 billion. CEO Nikesh Arora described the NGS ARR result as one of the highest beats in the company's history.

Palo Alto raised its full-year fiscal 2027 guidance, projecting adjusted earnings per share in the range of $4.26 to $4.30 and revenue between $14.52 billion and $14.57 billion. Both figures exceeded analyst expectations of $4.18 per share and $14.16 billion in revenue.

The Applied Compute deal represents a notable expansion beyond Palo Alto's core cybersecurity business. The startup helps enterprises run and customize their own AI models, and Arora framed the acquisition as a bet on the growing adoption of open-weight AI systems.

"If it works, I think this will dwarf the ability that we have in cybersecurity," Arora said on the earnings call. "This could be bigger than what we do."

"As AI reshapes the threat landscape, we must continually evolve to protect our customers and enable them to adopt AI securely," Arora added in a release.

Demand for Palo Alto's tools has been accelerating alongside broader fears about AI-powered cyberattacks, heightened by incidents including the Hugging Face breach. Palo Alto shares have gained 85% this year, reflecting sustained investor confidence in the cybersecurity sector's growth trajectory.

Outages affecting Microsoft, banks, and the FAA over the past week have added to those concerns, even as U.S. officials have so far ruled out malicious activity. Arora said the rapid acceleration in patching is placing strain on dated infrastructure, and the company expects to issue tens of thousands of virtual patches by the end of the fiscal year.

"Whatever they do today is going to take three to six months, maybe nine months, depending on how complex and how tired their environment is," Arora said.

The Applied Compute acquisition underscores a broader strategy of using M&A to position Palo Alto at the center of enterprise AI infrastructure, not just its security perimeter. Whether the startup's AI compute platform can scale to rival the company's cybersecurity revenue base will be a central question analysts are likely to track in the quarters ahead.
