Amazon has taken its opposition to a New York City labor bill directly into its shopping app and website, serving pop-up notices and homepage banners to users warning that proposed city legislation "could add up to $5 per order."

The campaign targets Intro 518, known as the Delivery Protection Act, a measure that would require Amazon to employ its network of third-party delivery companies directly rather than contracting with them — a model the company has relied upon since 2018.

Amazon's in-app message warns that passage of the bill would force the company to reconsider its presence in the city entirely. "If this legislation passes, we'd no longer be able to work with them, and we'd be forced to consider moving our operations outside of the city," the notice states. "That would likely mean slower and more expensive delivery for millions of customers in New York City, while also threatening small businesses and their thousands of employees."

The pop-up notices and a stationary banner on the store homepage direct users to a form where they can contact their council member to oppose the legislation.

The move is unusual. Amazon regularly publishes policy positions on its company website, but it rarely deploys its e-commerce storefront as a vehicle for advocacy. Other technology companies, including Uber and Lyft, have used their own apps to push for or against legislation in the past.

The Delivery Protection Act was introduced in February and is currently awaiting review by City Council Speaker Julie Menin. New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani endorsed the bill in August, arguing that Amazon's subcontractor model "leaves workers vulnerable and corporations free to avoid accountability for reckless conditions on city streets." Mamdani has also contended the bill would lead to safer working conditions for drivers and give the city authority to "regulate last-mile operations."

The legislation has drawn strong support from labor organizations, including the International Brotherhood of Teamsters, which has sought to unionize Amazon delivery and warehouse workers.

Amazon, for its part, has lobbied heavily against the bill. Business groups have launched local advertising campaigns and warned they may sue the city if the measure passes.

At its core, the dispute centers on Amazon's last-mile logistics model — a network of thousands of small companies that often work exclusively for Amazon to handle final package delivery. Amazon built that structure to accelerate shipping speeds and retain tighter control over its logistics operations.

The company's decision to push its message through the shopping app — real estate that generates significant consumer engagement — signals the degree to which Amazon views the bill as a material threat to its New York City operations, and reflects a broader trend of large technology companies leveraging their platforms to shape public policy outcomes.
