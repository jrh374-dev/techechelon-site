BlackRock CEO Larry Fink cautioned Wednesday that halting data center construction in the United States would push the economy into a recession, linking the fate of AI infrastructure buildout directly to broader economic health.

"If we have a moratorium of some sort in this country, we will be in a recession," Fink told CNBC after BlackRock reported better-than-expected third-quarter earnings early Wednesday.

Fink's comments arrived as local opposition to data center development has become a notable flashpoint heading into the November 3 midterm elections. Communities across the country are raising concerns about rising utility costs, water consumption, environmental damage, and questions over investment transparency.

BlackRock shares advanced about 1% in late morning trading following the earnings release.

Fink pointed to the scale of capital deployment underlying AI infrastructure as the foundation of his economic warning. "So much of our economy is based on the buildout of this capex," he said, referring to the investments being made to develop artificial intelligence systems and the physical facilities that support them.

Wall Street analysts estimate total AI capital expenditures could climb above $1 trillion in 2027 and exceed $5 trillion by 2030, underscoring the degree to which the sector has become a structural driver of investment activity.

While acknowledging public fears around the data center boom, Fink pushed back on the premise behind much of the opposition. "Most of it is on misinformation," he said.

He argued that data centers represent a net positive for local economies, citing demand for construction workers, physical digital infrastructure, AI compute hardware, and energy infrastructure required to secure reliable grid power. "Data centers are not the problem. They're creating more new jobs than ever before," Fink said.

The remarks reflect a growing tension between the rapid pace of AI infrastructure expansion and the communities where that infrastructure is being built. Local officials and residents in several states have moved to restrict or delay data center projects, citing strain on power grids and municipal water supplies.

With midterm elections weeks away, the data center debate has taken on political dimensions that extend beyond zoning disputes, drawing in major financial figures who have a direct stake in the continued flow of capital into AI-related construction.

How lawmakers respond to constituent pressure on data center development—and whether any formal restrictions gain traction at the state or federal level—will be closely watched by investors and infrastructure developers tracking the next phase of AI capital deployment.
