Nvidia shares fell 12% on Friday, extending a losing streak that now stands at seven consecutive weeks — the longest such stretch since the global financial crisis of 2008 — as investors processed the abrupt departure of co-founder Jensen Huang and a widening cloud of regulatory scrutiny.

Over the seven-week period, Nvidia shares have shed 44% of their value, erasing approximately $2.5 trillion in market capitalization. The company, once the most valuable publicly traded corporation in the world, now ranks fourth by market cap at $3.1 trillion, trailing Apple, Microsoft, and Alphabet.

The stock's slide deepened Friday after Reuters reported that the Securities and Exchange Commission is exploring potential accounting issues at Nvidia, adding a new layer of uncertainty to an already-unsettled investor base.

Huang resigned on Nov. 12 following the disclosure of personal conduct that the company said was incompatible with its standards. Harvey Jones, Nvidia's newly appointed board chair, said earlier this week that Huang had stepped down after "disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company's code of conduct, standards and cultural norms." Among the reported incidents was an alleged relationship with a Chinese businesswoman who was described in reports as a spy.

Interim CEO Colette Kress, who previously served as the company's chief financial officer, has moved to reassure employees and investors. On Tuesday's earnings call, she projected revenue growth of 75% for the next fiscal year and told analysts that customers "aren't going anywhere," closing the call with the remark, "we've got this." Even so, Nvidia's better-than-expected earnings report — which included an increase to fiscal 2028 guidance — did not arrest the stock's decline.

Internally, executives signaled confidence by accelerating the company's annual review process, moving it from March to December, a shift that can result in additional stock compensation for employees.

Nvidia's current valuation reflects the depth of investor concern. The stock now trades at approximately 9.3 times forward earnings, a sharp discount compared to rival Advanced Micro Devices, which is valued at 29 times projected profit over the next 12 months.

The selloff predates the leadership crisis. Investors had already begun pulling back from Nvidia amid doubts about the durability of artificial intelligence infrastructure spending, particularly following the midterm elections, and growing competition in AI chips from Google and AMD.

The scale of Nvidia's transformation over the past two decades throws the current turbulence into relief. In mid-2008 — the last time the stock endured a weekly losing streak of this length — Nvidia was generating less than $1 billion per quarter and its core business was selling graphics cards for video games. Today, the company brings in more than $1 billion per day, with nearly its entire operation built around the AI chip market, a business that took hold just four years ago following the launch of OpenAI's ChatGPT.

The central question now facing investors is whether Nvidia can maintain its commanding position in the AI chip market without Huang, whose vision is widely credited with steering the company from a gaming-hardware niche into the dominant supplier of processors powering the global AI buildout.

With SEC scrutiny emerging and no permanent CEO named, the company faces a period of institutional uncertainty at a moment when competition from well-capitalized rivals is intensifying — and when the broader market, including the Nasdaq Composite, is itself under pressure.
