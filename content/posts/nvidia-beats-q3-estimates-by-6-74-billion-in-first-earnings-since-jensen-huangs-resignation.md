Nvidia reported third-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue of $115.84 billion on Tuesday, topping analyst expectations of $109.10 billion, in the chipmaker's first earnings since co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as CEO last week amid a widening affair scandal.

Adjusted earnings per share came in at $2.68, beating the $2.48 consensus estimate, while revenue more than doubled from $57 billion in the year-earlier period, underscoring continued strength in Nvidia's core data center business.

Interim CEO Colette Kress, who has served as the company's chief financial officer for 13 years, raised full-year guidance on the earnings call, saying Nvidia now expects 75% year-over-year growth in fiscal year 2028—above the company's prior forecast of 70% and ahead of the $125.1 billion average analyst estimate for the current quarter's forward projection of roughly $130 billion.

Kress told investors it is "business as usual at Nvidia," emphasizing the depth of the company's engineering bench. "Remember, we have the best technologists on the planet with a world-class multi-year roadmap," she said on the call.

In her first interview since Huang's departure, Kress appeared on CNBC's "Mad Money" and told host Jim Cramer that demand for Nvidia's AI chips continues to accelerate, with hyperscalers "feverishly looking" for supply. "The most important thing we have seen is growth in the demand that's out there," she said. "Customers continue to be with us."

Kress also signaled a shift in how Nvidia will manage its financial commitments going forward, saying the company would be "rigorously evaluating future investments, commitments, and guarantees" and would remain "disciplined in how we commit capital." The remarks came amid reports that Nvidia is looking to scale back backstop agreements that have supported the broader AI infrastructure industry—including a reported $105 billion backstop tied to a data center project planned for OpenAI under Huang.

Shares of Nvidia initially declined in extended trading after board chair Harvey Jones said the company would not pursue Huang's reinstatement as CEO. The stock later recovered, rising nearly 4% after-hours. Even so, Nvidia's stock remains down 24% this month and recently completed its sixth consecutive weekly decline—its longest losing streak since 2012—trading at its lowest level since July 2025.

Shares of neocloud operators CoreWeave, Nebius, and Iren each rose between 8% and 10% in extended trading following the results, reflecting confidence in the continued AI infrastructure buildout.

The board has engaged executive search firm Heidrick & Struggles to identify a permanent CEO. Huang said in a statement Tuesday that he remains open to returning, and an online petition calling for his reinstatement had gathered nearly 200,000 signatures as of Tuesday evening.

Nvidia faces additional headwinds beyond the leadership transition. The Commerce Department on Monday announced a change barring Nvidia's chips from being rented to Chinese companies, and the company said it is "not assuming any Data Center compute revenue from China" in its outlook. The company is also contending with broader concerns about an AI spending slowdown and shifting political dynamics following this month's midterm elections.

Gene Munster, managing partner of Deepwater Asset Management, said investors should focus on Nvidia's business outlook while the board conducts its CEO search. "The current business and the outlook is rock solid," Munster said.

With strong results now on the books and guidance raised, the near-term focus turns to the CEO search—and whether a new permanent leader can maintain the operational momentum Kress has signaled while pulling back on the expansive financial commitments Huang made during his tenure.
