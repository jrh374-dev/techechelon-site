For years, the relationship between hyperscale cloud operators and electric utilities ran on a quiet bargain: utilities would interconnect data center loads in 18 to 24 months, and data centers would behave like predictable industrial customers. Both sides got what they needed.

That arrangement is finished.

In the last twelve months, the largest cloud operators have begun permitting their own substations, signing decade-long nuclear power purchase agreements, and lobbying state public service commissions for fast-track interconnection rules. Three of the top five are exploring direct merchant power generation. None of this is contemplated in the utility planning frameworks regulators were operating with as recently as 2024.

## Why the timeline broke

AI compute demand has grown faster than any prior wave of electricity demand on record. A single hyperscaler campus now requests one to two gigawatts of new capacity in a market where annual peak demand growth for an entire state utility might be 600 megawatts. The capacity simply does not exist on the timeline the cloud operators need it.

## What changes

Expect the next round of data-center siting decisions to be driven by transmission capacity, not by latency to end users. Texas, Virginia, and Arizona — the three states with the most permissive interconnection rules — will dominate. New England and California, despite being the largest end markets, will see almost no new build.
