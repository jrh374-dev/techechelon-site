Nvidia shares were trading 0.56% lower as of early Tuesday morning, extending a multi-day decline that has seen the chip giant's stock drop to its lowest close since July 1, 2025, as investors weigh the consequences of Jensen Huang's resignation and escalating U.S.-China technology restrictions.

Huang resigned last week after Nvidia's board announced it had received reports that he had a relationship with a woman U.S. intelligence reportedly believes to be a Chinese spy. Huang disclosed the affair to employees in an email on Nov. 8. The stock slid 4% on Monday alone.

An online petition calling for Huang's reinstatement drew more than 190,000 signatures, including one from Elon Musk. However, a person familiar with the matter told CNBC on Monday that Nvidia's board is not considering bringing back the former CEO.

The leadership turmoil arrives as Nvidia faces growing scrutiny from U.S. lawmakers over Chinese companies' access to its advanced chips, which existing export controls already restrict. On Monday, the U.S. Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security announced an interim final rule designed to prevent Chinese companies from remotely accessing AI chips through overseas data centers, a known method for circumventing export restrictions.

The moves reflect a broader strategic debate that Huang himself had been central to. He had argued that keeping U.S. technology available in China could help the U.S. maintain its lead in the artificial intelligence race. The opposing view, now gaining institutional momentum, holds that tighter controls are necessary to prevent adversaries from closing the gap.

That tension sits at the heart of a widening dilemma for U.S. technology companies. "Companies operating in China face competing pressures from Washington and Beijing," New York-based consulting firm Strategy Risks said in a client brief following the Nvidia scandal. "Actions that satisfy one government can create serious problems with the other, putting market access, investments, and corporate reputation at risk."

The diplomatic backdrop adds urgency to the question. On Sunday, nine countries — including Singapore and the United Kingdom — signed the U.S. State Department's "San Francisco Declaration" to safeguard sensitive technologies. The signing came days before President Donald Trump is set to meet Chinese leader Xi Jinping. U.S. officials said Trump does not plan to raise the alleged Nvidia spy case during Thursday's meeting, and unlike prior summits in September and May, Trump is not bringing a business delegation this time.

Apple is navigating similar terrain. John Ternus, who took over as Apple CEO from Tim Cook in October, faces scrutiny over how the company manages its deep manufacturing ties to China. Apple still manufactures most of its iPhones in China, and Greater China — which Apple defines as mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan — accounts for around 15% of its sales.

Kyle Chan, a fellow at The Brookings Institution, described Nvidia and Apple as "stark outliers" against a broader trend of American tech companies diversifying away from China. Chan noted that Ternus "may accelerate a process of diversification away from China that Tim Cook started," but cautioned that "it's hard to imagine that China will not play an essential role in Apple's future under Ternus, given the depth and breadth of China's manufacturing supply chains."

Complicating any U.S. pivot away from China is Beijing's grip on rare earth minerals and critical supply chains. China has amassed control of the supply chain for critical minerals and has used that leverage in trade talks with both the U.S. and the European Union. Cameron Johnson, a Shanghai-based senior partner at business consultancy Tidalwave Solutions, warned of looming shortages of specific materials, including yttrium, which is essential for airplane engine production and repair.

"There's just no road map. That's really one of the biggest issues," Johnson said. "Companies have no idea what they're ordering now, whether they will have access to it again."

Nvidia is scheduled to report earnings soon, and investors are watching closely for how incoming leadership characterizes the company's China strategy — a question that has taken on new weight since Huang's departure.
