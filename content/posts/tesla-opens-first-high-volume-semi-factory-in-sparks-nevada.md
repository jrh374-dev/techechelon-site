Tesla officially opened its first dedicated high-volume production facility for its Semi electric truck in Sparks, Nevada, on Thursday, marking a significant expansion of the company's push into commercial freight as CEO Elon Musk targets the heavy-duty trucking market.

The ribbon-cutting event, livestreamed at 9 p.m. ET, follows more than eight years since Tesla first unveiled the Semi in 2017. The company began delivering the trucks in limited quantities to early customers, including anchor client PepsiCo, in 2022.

Tesla aims to ramp Semi production to high volumes by next year, positioning itself in a commercial trucking segment where soaring diesel costs have made the case for electric alternatives more compelling.

The factory opening arrives as Tesla's core electric vehicle business faces intensifying global competition and as the company continues to struggle for traction with its Cybertruck, a smaller angular-steel pickup that reached customers in 2023.

Recent order activity signals growing interest in the Semi. On Tuesday, industry coalition ZET SCALE announced it had selected Tesla as its primary original equipment manufacturer for an initial order of 2,500 trucks, with plans to expand purchases beyond 10,000 electric trucks across multiple providers. Trucking firm Einride also recently confirmed an agreement for 500 Tesla Semi trucks, with some deployments planned for this month.

Self-driving capability remains a central part of Tesla's longer-term pitch for the Semi. On the company's earnings call in July, Musk told investors that its Full Self-Driving system would reach the Semi "probably around the end of this year or early next year," though he declined to commit to a specific date.

"There just aren't enough people around who want to drive trucks, which are crucial for transport throughout America," Musk said at the time. "An autonomous Semi is actually going to be very important to address the shortage of truck drivers."

Autonomous trucking rivals Aurora Innovation and Kodiak AI are already conducting driverless tests and hauling commercial cargo on public roads, giving them a head start in the race to deploy fully uncrewed freight operations.

For now, Tesla's primary competitive selling point is battery range. The long-range Semi is rated at an estimated 500 miles per full charge, while its standard variant is rated at an estimated 325 miles, according to the company. Tesla also plans to bring the Semi to the European market, where competitors including Volvo and Daimler Truck already offer battery-powered commercial models.

According to a 2025 report from the International Council on Clean Transportation, trucks and buses represent less than 10% of road vehicles globally but account for nearly half of transport-related air pollution — a statistic the industry frequently cites in making the environmental case for electrification.

Whether the Nevada factory can deliver the production scale Tesla has promised — and whether autonomous capability arrives on the timeline Musk outlined — will be closely watched by fleet operators, investors, and a commercial trucking industry weighing an increasingly costly transition away from diesel.
