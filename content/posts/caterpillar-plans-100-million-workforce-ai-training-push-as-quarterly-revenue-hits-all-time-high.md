Caterpillar plans to spend $100 million over the next five years training its 118,000 employees in artificial intelligence, autonomy, and robotics, as the industrial equipment maker applies lessons from decades of mining automation to its broader AI deployment strategy.

The announcement, made by Caterpillar Chief Technology Officer Jaime Mineart in remarks to TechCrunch on the sidelines of the Ai4 conference in Las Vegas, underscores how established industrial companies are increasingly treating workforce transformation as a core capital commitment — not just a technology problem.

"The hard part about autonomy and about physical AI is incorporating that technology into the customer jobsite and into the workflows," Mineart said.

Caterpillar's push into autonomous systems began in mining, where labor shortages and hazardous working conditions made automation a practical necessity. The company today sells automated haul trucks, drilling equipment, underground loaders, dozers, and remote-controlled construction machines, alongside a software command center, fleet management tools, and remote terrain intelligence capabilities.

Mineart said the company is now extending that playbook into what she described as "much more dynamic environments" — jobsites, quarries, and construction sites — where conditions are less predictable than controlled mining operations.

One concrete product of that expansion is the Cat AI Assistant, a voice-command tool that lets field technicians standing next to a machine pull up repair procedures, troubleshoot problems, and identify parts before beginning a repair. Mineart said the tool is now in active use by customers, operators, and technicians.

The assistant draws on Caterpillar's proprietary data pool, which Mineart said encompasses about 1.6 million connected assets globally and more than 16 petabytes of structured data.

Beyond field service, Caterpillar is deploying AI to power site-scanning software, generate digital twins in manufacturing, and accelerate software development. "We use AI agents to modernize legacy code, generate and test new software, and identify defects earlier," Mineart said.

As machines grow more autonomous, Mineart acknowledged a structural shift in how operators work — some will move from controlling a single machine directly to overseeing multiple machines from a remote command center. That transition is part of what the $100 million training program is designed to address.

Mineart also emphasized that Caterpillar relies on experienced operators to help train AI systems, treating institutional knowledge built over decades as a data asset rather than an obsolete input.

The AI investment comes as Caterpillar's underlying business is benefiting from the broader infrastructure buildout surrounding artificial intelligence. The company's quarterly revenue reached an all-time high of $20.5 billion in the second quarter, driven in part by strong demand for power-generation equipment used in data centers. Its power-generation division saw sales spike 72% to $3.10 billion in that period.

CEO Joe Creed said demand for cloud computing and generative AI infrastructure shows no sign of slowing. "No one is slowing down," Creed said.

The $100 million training commitment signals that Caterpillar views AI deployment as an ongoing organizational change rather than a one-time technology integration — a distinction that will likely be watched closely by other large industrial companies navigating similar transitions.
