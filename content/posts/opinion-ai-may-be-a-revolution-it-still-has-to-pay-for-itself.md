The global stock market correction in October 2026 raises a fundamental question: is the artificial intelligence investment boom beginning to face its own economic limits? Behind the optimism that AI will increase global productivity lies a race to build data centers, purchase semiconductors, and finance infrastructure on an ever-larger scale.

According to Crisil Integral IQ, in an analysis published October 8, 2026, the combined capital expenditures of the six largest hyperscaler companies are estimated to reach around $1.2 trillion by 2027. The issue is not merely the size of the investment, but whether AI revenues and cash flows can grow quickly enough to justify such expenditures. The greatest risk is not that AI will fail to change the world, but that AI investment will generate returns smaller than market expectations.

## When expectations begin to be tested

On [October 14, 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/13/stock-market-today-live-updates-.html), the Nasdaq fell 1.88 percent, the S&P 500 declined 1.11 percent, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 4.3 percent. Oracle shares fell 8.8 percent, while CoreWeave plunged 15 percent. Higher-than-expected U.S. inflation increased concerns that the Federal Reserve would keep interest rates high or tighten monetary policy again.

Pressure continued that week. By the close on October 16, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index had [declined for eight consecutive sessions](https://www.investing.com/indices/phlx-semiconductor-historical-data). Asian markets also weakened when trading resumed on October 19, with Japan's Nikkei down 2 percent and South Korea's stock market down 1.6 percent, as concerns over AI's capital requirements, rising funding costs, and geopolitical tensions converged.

These movements do not yet prove that the AI bubble has burst. However, they show that investors are beginning to question whether profit growth will match the scale of investment. This demonstrates an investment paradox: a powerful technology does not necessarily constitute a profitable investment.

In present value theory, stock prices reflect the present value of future cash flows. If profit expectations decline or the discount rate rises, stock valuations can correct even if companies' long-term prospects remain promising.

AI faces a dual test. Companies must prove that AI services generate sustainable revenues while also covering the costs of chips, data centers, electricity, cooling, and financing. According to a [Reuters analysis](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/07/22/analysis-ai-investment-boom-puts-big-tech039s-free-cash-flow-under-pressure) of Big Tech cash-flow pressures published July 22, 2026, capital expenditures by five major technology companies could exceed their combined free cash flow in 2027, based on analyst projections cited in the report. This is a projection, not a certainty, but it is sufficient to explain why investors are increasingly paying attention to capital efficiency.

Overinvestment theory is also relevant. When companies compete to dominate a market, each is driven to build capacity as quickly as possible so as not to fall behind competitors. If all companies do the same, industry capacity may grow faster than demand capable of generating profits. As a result, expensive data centers may operate below optimal capacity, while new generations of chips cause previous hardware to lose economic value more rapidly.

## Debt can magnify the correction

The issue becomes more serious when AI expansion increasingly depends on debt financing. Crisil Integral IQ notes that debt issuance by the five major hyperscalers reached around $218 billion during 2026 through early October. At the same time, their combined capital expenditures are projected to reach $733 billion in 2026. These figures reveal an important shift: AI investment is no longer funded solely by internal cash, but is increasingly connected to the bond market and external financing.

In financial accelerator theory, declining asset values and deteriorating revenue expectations can tighten access to credit. Borrowing costs rise, new projects become less viable, and companies are forced to reduce investment. If such pressures spread, the impact could spill over from technology stocks to corporate bonds, semiconductor suppliers, electric utilities, and global stock markets. Portfolio diversification could also become less effective if various assets turn out to depend on the same AI investment cycle.

## Energy becomes a physical and economic constraint

The AI boom also requires enormous amounts of electricity. Data centers require stable energy supplies, cooling systems, and high-capacity power grids. When energy prices rise, operating costs increase and project viability may deteriorate. At the same time, geopolitical tensions could constrain oil supplies and push inflation higher. If inflation persists, central banks will have less room to cut interest rates. This condition puts pressure on the valuations of assets that rely on long-term profits.

Therefore, war and energy turmoil can reinforce a correction in AI investment, but they are not automatically its primary cause. The root of the problem remains the relationship between the amount of capital invested, the cost of financing, and the ability to generate cash flow.

## The lesson of the dot-com era

Thus, a tentative conclusion can be drawn: the AI bubble has not been proven, but its risks are sufficiently real. The lesson from the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s is that revolutionary technology does not guarantee that every investment within it will be profitable. The internet continued to change the world after the bubble burst, but many companies built during the period of euphoria failed to survive.

AI could face a similar paradox. The technology may generate major productivity gains, while some investments made too early or at excessively high valuations may nevertheless end in losses. Therefore, the important indicators to monitor are not only stock prices, but also AI revenue growth, free cash flow, debt ratios, interest costs, data center utilization rates, and investment returns.

If profits grow in line with capital expenditures, the current correction could become a healthy adjustment. However, if investment continues to rise while monetization lags behind, the market risks facing a deeper valuation correction and broader credit pressures. AI may be a technological revolution. However, a technological revolution does not exempt investors from the laws of economics: capital must generate returns commensurate with the risks and costs borne.
