Google drew sharp criticism from lawmakers, regulators, and local officials on Tuesday after announcing that schools using its K-12 software suite would lose access to Search unless they opt in to the company's artificial intelligence tools.

Chris Phillips, vice president for education at Google, told The New York Times that "Search and AI are deeply integrated," and that students whose schools do not opt in to AI products such as Gemini "will not have access to search, and they'll see a message from their school that indicates it's not available."

The announcement, [first reported by TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/04/google-faces-backlash-for-plan-that-would-turn-off-search-for-schools-that-restrict-ai), landed on Election Day and prompted immediate condemnation from figures across the political spectrum.

Senator Bernie Sanders (I-VT) accused the company of "extortion" in a post on X, writing that "Google is threatening schools: if you stop your students from using AI, we will cut off your access to Google Search."

Senator Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) said on X that Google is "more concerned with forcing Gemini into schools than enabling students to succeed."

Lina Khan, the former chair of the Federal Trade Commission, framed the move in antitrust terms. "This highlights the danger of allowing an unregulated monopolist to control an essential service," she wrote. "Multiple courts have found Google liable for violating antitrust laws, and this latest move shows it remains unchained." New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani echoed that characterization, writing that "no corporation should have the power to dictate how we teach our children."

Educators have raised particular concern about Google's AI overviews in Search, which some argue could discourage students from independently working through problems. The concern is compounded by the fact that millions of students rely on Chromebooks and Google's education platform, leaving alternative search engines beyond easy reach.

In response to the backlash, Google clarified its position in a post on X, stating that Gemini, Gemini Notebook, and Search would be turned off by default, and that schools could enable each product individually. The company framed the change as expanding choice rather than restricting it.

"We know there's concern about students using AI to shortcut schoolwork, and schools have told us they want control," Google said in a statement. "That's exactly why we are making these changes today to address the feedback we've heard from educators in recent months. And we'll continue to engage deeply with educators to ensure we are giving schools more choice, more controls, and improved Search for learning."

Google also said it is developing a "Guided Learning" tool for student accounts, designed to walk students through the process of solving a problem rather than providing direct answers — steering a student struggling with a math question toward underlying concepts, for example, rather than surfacing a finished solution.

The episode comes as Democrats, following a midterm sweep that secured control of both chambers, have signaled that Big Tech regulation and AI oversight will be top legislative priorities — a backdrop that could intensify congressional scrutiny of moves like Google's school announcement.

Whether Google's opt-in clarification defuses the political pressure remains to be seen, as the company continues to face active antitrust litigation that courts have already ruled against it on, leaving its next moves in the education market under heightened public watch.
