At least six members of Congress or their immediate families purchased SpaceX shares within six days of the company's June 12 initial public offering, according to a tally of House financial disclosures — transactions that ethics experts say expose the tension between lawmakers' official duties and their personal financial interests.

The purchases totaled between roughly $83,000 and $245,000 combined, reflecting the ranges that federal disclosure rules require members to report rather than exact figures.

The buyers were Reps. William Timmons, R-S.C.; John McGuire, R-Va.; Dan Meuser, R-Pa.; Gil Cisneros, D-Calif.; John James, R-Mich.; and Jared Moskowitz, D-Fla. Three of the transactions were made by a spouse or dependent child rather than the member directly.

Timmons, who chairs a House oversight panel on military and foreign affairs and sits on a financial services subcommittee covering artificial intelligence, purchased between $50,001 and $100,000 in SpaceX shares three days after the IPO. Cisneros, who sits on the House Armed Services Committee, which oversees Pentagon and Space Force contracts, bought between $1,001 and $15,000 in his own name on June 18.

James' wife purchased between $15,001 and $50,000 in SpaceX stock on June 12, the day of the IPO, according to a financial disclosure filed this month. James sits on the House Energy and Commerce Committee, which plays a regulatory role over the energy grid and AI data center development — a sector in which SpaceX subsidiary SpaceXAI has been an active builder. "The James children are excited about space exploration and Liz purchased stock for them through a brokerage firm, like other members of the public were welcome to do," a spokesperson for James said in an emailed statement.

McGuire's wife invested between $1,001 and $15,000 on June 15. A dependent child of Meuser's bought between $15,001 and $50,000 on the same date.

There is no evidence that any of the lawmakers traded on nonpublic information or violated congressional trading rules. The transactions are legal, and some members say outside advisors manage their portfolios.

Still, ethics experts argue the purchases raise concerns that extend beyond the question of insider trading. "The potential conflict of interest exists when the committee assignment may overlap with this company as a government contractor," said Kedric Payne, ethics director at the nonprofit, nonpartisan Campaign Legal Center. SpaceX relies heavily on federal approvals and receives billions in government contracts — spending that falls within the jurisdiction of several of the committees on which these members serve.

Payne added that the family-member dimension of some trades does not reduce those concerns. "The core principle of financial disclosure forms since the very beginning, decades ago, is that the financial interest of the spouse and a dependent child are exactly the same as the financial interest of the member," he said.

Rep. Pramila Jayapal, D-Wash., was more direct. "Members are making decisions, buying and selling as if they're on Wall Street. And they're not doing it in the interest of their constituents," Jayapal told CNBC when asked about the SpaceX trades. "They're doing it in the interest of their pockets."

Five of the six members identified serve on committees whose work intersects with SpaceX's defense, satellite communications, AI, federal contracting, or securities businesses. Spokespeople for Timmons, McGuire, and Meuser did not respond to requests for comment. Cisneros said in a statement earlier this month that he does not manage the "day-to-day trading" of his investment portfolio; his office did not subsequently respond to questions about potential conflicts of interest.

Monday was the deadline for reporting IPO-day purchases; trades made in the six days following the offering are due by August 2, meaning the total number of congressional buyers could rise.

The episode is drawing renewed attention to the broader question of whether members of Congress should be permitted to trade individual stocks at all — and it may be a preview of sharper debates to come, with highly anticipated offerings from companies including OpenAI and Anthropic still on the horizon.
