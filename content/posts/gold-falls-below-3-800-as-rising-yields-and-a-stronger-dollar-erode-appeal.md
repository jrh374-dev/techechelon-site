Gold dropped below $3,800 per ounce on Wednesday for the first time since September 2025, extending a sharp decline that has erased roughly a third of the metal's value since it set a historic high of $5,589.38 on January 28.

The retreat marks a dramatic reversal for a commodity that hit new records 53 times in 2025 before peaking in January of this year.

A convergence of macroeconomic forces is now working against the metal, according to Suki Cooper, global head of commodities research at Standard Chartered. "Gold can behave as a commodity or a currency," Cooper said, adding that it is currently the currency dynamic — not the commodity one — driving prices lower.

Cooper traced gold's earlier climb to strong physical demand that began building in 2022, followed by a second phase in which investors reconsidered the metal's place in their portfolios as the cost of holding a non-yielding asset declined. ETF holdings, led by the SPDR Gold Shares fund, rose by roughly 800 tons in 2025 and helped absorb excess supply. By year-end 2025, net ETF holdings had surpassed 4,000 tons globally, and gold ETFs recorded their strongest year ever for inflows — $89 billion — with total assets more than doubling to $559 billion, according to the World Gold Council.

That environment has since shifted considerably. Federal Reserve Board of Governors member Christopher Waller said this week that a 50-basis-point rate hike may be necessary to "shock people," reinforcing expectations of a more aggressive tightening path.

Evan Mills, a financial advisor with Scholar Financial Advising, a fee-only firm in Winston-Salem, North Carolina, said the current backdrop is effectively the inverse of what drove gold higher. "In 2026, the Fed has leaned more toward higher interest rates," Mills said. "There are higher Treasury yields and a strengthening dollar, so the opportunity cost of holding an asset that doesn't produce income is much higher."

Mills noted that money market funds now offer yields of 4% to 5%, making cash a credible alternative. "Cash maintains purchasing power, and you don't need to put it in an illiquid asset or one where you're relying on another person to see the value," he said.

The sell-off accelerated after January, when the speed of the rally made some investors uneasy. Cooper noted that a subsequent Middle East conflict shifted gold's role from portfolio safe haven to "a liquidity provider" — investors sold the metal to raise cash, triggering the first leg of the correction. Rate expectations have driven the decline since.

Silver has also fallen sharply from its own all-time high this year, underscoring the broader pressure on precious metals in a rising-rate environment.

For investors already holding gold, Mills urged perspective over panic. "When expectations are 20% or 30% returns in a year, then of course when returns drop, people start to panic," he said. He cautioned against viewing gold as a growth vehicle. "Gold is insurance, not an engine, and the mistake is buying insurance when its price has skyrocketed," Mills said.

He added that investors who purchased the metal at January's peak as a modest, long-term hedge have little cause for alarm. "You have nothing to really worry about, because that's what the whole goal of gold was."

Looking ahead, Mills said further Fed rate hikes and a likely strengthening dollar present continued near-term headwinds. With real yields rising and the classic stock-and-bond portfolio becoming more attractive by comparison, gold's path back toward its January highs appears narrow — at least until the monetary policy calculus shifts again.
