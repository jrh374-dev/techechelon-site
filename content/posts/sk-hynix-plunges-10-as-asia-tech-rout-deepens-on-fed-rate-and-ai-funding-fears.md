A broad sell-off swept through Asian technology stocks Thursday, with South Korean chipmaker SK Hynix falling more than 10% as investors weighed the prospect of further Federal Reserve interest rate increases against the enormous capital requirements of building out artificial intelligence infrastructure.

Samsung Electronics shed 7.1%, while other South Korean chip-related names followed suit. LG Innotek sank 8.1%, Seoul Semiconductor dropped 7.7%, and Samsung SDI declined 6.8%.

The damage extended into Japan. SoftBank Group tumbled 7.9%, semiconductor testing equipment maker Advantest lost 6.9%, chipmaking equipment supplier Tokyo Electron fell 5.4%, and Renesas Electronics declined 5.4%.

The Asian declines tracked a punishing session on Wall Street the prior day, where hotter-than-expected U.S. inflation data stoked fears of additional rate hikes. The Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.88% to close at 26,692.87, the S&P 500 shed 1.11% to end at 7,718.94, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped 0.09%.

At the center of investor concern is the cost of financing the infrastructure buildout underpinning the AI investment cycle. With U.S. Treasury yields climbing above 5%, higher borrowing costs threaten to pressure the capital spending plans of companies across the AI supply chain.

Chief executives at Broadcom and Intel warned on Monday that financing could become a significant headwind to AI expansion. Broadcom's Hock Tan said the funding challenge could prove a bigger obstacle than shortages of chips or electricity.

Dan Ives, tech analyst at Yorkville Ives, acknowledged the concern but stopped short of calling it a fundamental threat to AI demand. "Higher yields raise the cost of financing these builds, and investors are right to focus on balance sheets and contract structure, but rates are a financing question, not a demand question," Ives said in a note.

Ives argued the pullback represented a buying opportunity, maintaining that the AI infrastructure build remained in its early stages. "What would change our view is a demand signal, not a rate signal," he said, pointing to potential cuts in hyperscaler capital expenditure plans, canceled contracts, or falling GPU rental prices as the warning signs worth watching.

For now, the sell-off reflects a market recalibrating around a scenario that was largely absent from AI investment theses formed in a lower-rate environment — one in which the cost of money itself becomes a constraint on building the infrastructure AI requires.

Whether that recalibration proves temporary or signals a more durable reset in AI-related valuations will likely depend on incoming data from both the Federal Reserve and the hyperscalers that have driven the bulk of AI capital spending.
