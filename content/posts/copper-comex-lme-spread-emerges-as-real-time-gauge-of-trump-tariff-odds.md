A niche arbitrage trade in the copper market has quietly transformed into one of Wall Street's closest proxies for U.S. tariff policy, as investors use the price gap between American and London futures to price in the probability of fresh levies on refined copper.

Copper futures reached a record high of nearly $6.90 per pound last week, reflecting more than a year of sustained price gains driven in part by surging demand from AI infrastructure buildouts, grid modernization projects, and defense spending — all of which rely heavily on the industrial metal.

At the center of the shift is the spread between U.S. COMEX copper futures and London Metal Exchange prices. Historically, the gap was exploited by physical traders, banks, hedge funds, producers, and consumers to profit from temporary price differences between the two markets, driven by factors such as Chinese demand shocks or South American supply disruptions.

That calculus has changed. Analysts at Societe Generale now say the spread has been upended by the prospect of Section 232 tariffs on refined copper, with the COMEX premium increasingly functioning as a market-implied probability of further U.S. duties.

"The COMEX-LME spread has increasingly become a gauge of U.S. tariff expectations, with a wider premium signaling greater perceived tariff risk and continuing to pull metal into the U.S.," Ewa Manthey, commodities strategist at ING, told CNBC via email.

The Commerce Department has already recommended a phased universal tariff structure: a 15% duty on refined copper effective January 1, 2027, rising to 30% on January 1, 2028. The U.S. currently charges a 50% levy on imports of semi-finished copper products and certain other copper-containing goods.

To translate the spread into actionable odds, SocGen analysts led by Mike Haigh, head of FIC and commodity research, modeled the cost of moving LME-grade copper from European warehouses to the U.S. East Coast, then compared that all-in delivered price against COMEX futures. The current premium implies a 14.6% probability of the 15% phased tariff taking effect in January 2027, rising to a 37% probability of the 30% duty by January 2028.

The tariff uncertainty has already reshaped physical trade flows. The U.S. imported more than 200,000 metric tons of copper in July — its highest monthly intake in 12 years — as traders moved to front-run potential levies.

Natalie Scott-Gray, senior metals demand strategist at StoneX, described the overdue Section 232 decision on refined copper as the "single biggest catalyst" facing the copper market. In a recent market commentary, Scott-Gray noted that comprehensive tariffs would squeeze supply available outside the U.S., while a decision against tariffs would unwind the arbitrage premium entirely.

SocGen analysts said U.S. policymakers have grown increasingly concerned about the country's reliance on imported refined copper, framing the Section 232 probe as part of a broader effort at "securing access to a material seen as critical to both economic growth and national security."

Manthey noted that a wider premium remains supportive for near-term copper prices, "particularly as mine supply remains tight and competition for available metal between the U.S. and China intensifies," before adding that "tariff uncertainty means volatility is likely to stay elevated."

With a final White House decision still pending, traders and analysts say the COMEX-LME spread will remain the most direct, continuously updated signal of where U.S. copper tariff policy is headed — and what it will cost when it arrives.
