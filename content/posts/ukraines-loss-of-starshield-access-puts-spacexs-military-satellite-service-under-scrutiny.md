Ukraine's sudden loss of full access to Starshield, SpaceX's military-grade satellite network, has renewed scrutiny of who controls the system and whether the White House played a role in restricting the technology as Russia intensified attacks on Kyiv.

Officials in Kyiv said SpaceX imposed restrictions on Starshield without advance notice, disrupting military operations and hampering efforts to protect civilians during a wave of intense Russian strikes this week.

SpaceX CEO Elon Musk responded by shifting responsibility for any changes to the U.S. government, saying that Starshield systems are owned and controlled by Washington, with the U.S. military and ultimately President Donald Trump determining any limits on their use. The White House and the Pentagon did not immediately reply to requests for comment.

The episode has opened a fresh rift between Trump and Senate Republicans. GOP senators are scrambling to determine whether Trump played a role in reducing Ukraine's defensive capabilities, according to reporting from The Hill, with many of Ukraine's staunchest supporters within the Senate Republican Conference staying silent as of Wednesday.

At its core, the dispute centers on what Starshield is and how it differs from SpaceX's commercial Starlink service. Starshield is SpaceX's satellite network designed specifically for government and national security use, built on the same technology and launch infrastructure as Starlink. It currently focuses on Earth observation, secure global communications for government users, and hosted payloads — meaning it can accommodate specialized equipment aboard its satellites for customers.

Unlike Starlink, which serves consumers and businesses with high-speed internet access in more than 160 countries, territories, and other markets, Starshield features enhanced security capabilities, including high-assurance cryptographic systems designed to handle classified information. Its terminals are specialized, and its satellites can connect with one another through laser communications technology.

SpaceX has secured over $6 billion in multi-year U.S. government contracts for Starshield, primarily from two major Space Force contracts covering low Earth orbit-based communications and sensing constellations, according to the company's latest earnings report.

Musk drew a clear line between the two services on Wednesday, saying commercial Starlink is restricted to civilian and defensive purposes and cannot be used for offensive military operations. Starshield, by contrast, operates under government authority — a distinction that now carries significant geopolitical weight given Kyiv's complaint that the restrictions arrived without warning.

Starlink operates in more than 160 countries and territories, offering residential plans starting at $55 per month, with download speeds exceeding 400 megabits per second in most locations, according to the company. Starshield relies on that same satellite network but adds layers of government-grade security and dedicated user equipment on top of it.

The Ukraine access dispute comes amid an already strained relationship between Trump and Senate Republicans over the administration's approach to the war. The Starshield episode adds a technical and corporate dimension to that tension, underscoring the degree to which U.S. military satellite infrastructure — contracted through a private company whose CEO holds significant influence in Washington — can become a lever in foreign policy decisions.

How the administration responds to congressional pressure, and whether it offers any formal explanation for the reported restrictions, will likely shape the next phase of the standoff between the White House and GOP members who have supported continued aid to Ukraine.
