SpaceX's Starlink and Starshield satellite internet service remained throttled in Ukraine as of Friday, with Ukrainian officials and a prominent national security expert contradicting President Donald Trump's repeated assertions that service had been restored.

The restrictions, which Ukrainian officials say have been in place since Oct. 14, impose speed limits on Starshield terminals — the military-grade version of Starlink — that cut off drone connections when the aircraft exceed 100 mph, disrupting combat missions.

"Every day matters for Ukraine, because every Ukrainian life matters," wrote Denys Sienik, Ukraine's deputy ambassador to the U.S., in a Friday post on X. "Unfortunately, as of today, nothing has changed. We continue to wait."

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy confirmed the restrictions had not been lifted. "The speed has not been restored," Zelenskyy wrote on X Friday, adding that Ukraine was also still awaiting the delivery of missile interceptors "that were already agreed on." "Things have become harder — partly because of the Starlink and Starshield policies and speed limits," he wrote.

Zelenskyy's defense tech advisor, Serhii Beskrestnov, echoed that assessment on his Telegram channel. "While there are isolated cases where Starlink may operate somewhat beyond the established limits, this is likely a result of SpaceX's algorithms or specific usage conditions, rather than an actual removal of restrictions," Beskrestnov said in a post translated by Google.

The on-the-ground reports stand in direct conflict with Trump's public statements. On Thursday, Trump indicated service had been restored, saying of Elon Musk, "The bottom line, he put it back." But by Friday, Trump told reporters outside the White House that he had not in fact spoken with Musk about the issue. "I've heard it was fixed," Trump said. "But if it's not, I'll speak to him."

Dmitri Alperovitch, chairman of the Silverado Policy Accelerator and a co-founder and former chief technology officer of CrowdStrike, told CNBC that the operational consequences were tangible. "They are losing drones," Alperovitch said. "Ability to do mid strike to hit Russian logistics is now limited."

Who authorized the throttling remains unclear. Musk and SpaceX representatives did not respond to requests for comment. Democratic lawmakers have called for an investigation, while a bipartisan group sent a letter to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on Thursday urging the agency to "act expeditiously to expand its Starshield arrangements and ensure uninterrupted satellite communications support for Ukraine."

Facing continued uncertainty over Starlink access, Ukraine has begun exploring alternative providers. Zelenskyy said Wednesday that he met with Norwegian officials and defense company Kongsberg to discuss air defense systems and new satellite communication options. On Thursday, Ukraine's Ministry of Defense signed a letter of intent with Nokia and Finnish space intelligence firm ICEYE to "explore cooperation in space" and "support Ukraine's defense capabilities, particularly in building sovereign satellite communications."

"Modern warfare has proven that reliable communications are a matter of national security," Zelenskyy said in a statement. "We must all rely on capabilities that we can control."

Experts have cautioned, however, that replacing Starlink is not a near-term option. Comparable alternatives do not yet exist at the scale or performance level that Ukraine's military requires, leaving Kyiv in a position of continued dependence even as it hedges with letters of intent and diplomatic outreach.

With the conflict continuing and no confirmed resolution from SpaceX, the gap between Washington's public messaging and conditions on the ground in Ukraine underscores the fragility of a military communications architecture built around a single commercial provider.
