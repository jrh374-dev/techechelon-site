U.S. employee engagement fell to 29% in the third quarter of 2026, its lowest point since 2011, according to Gallup's quarterly workforce poll — a two-percentage-point drop from the first half of the year that erases much of the progress made in the years leading up to the pandemic.

Each percentage point in the survey represents roughly 1.6 million U.S. employees, meaning approximately 3.2 million fewer workers were actively engaged in their jobs quarter over quarter.

The decline was concentrated among remote and hybrid workers, particularly those at organizations that have adopted artificial intelligence, Gallup said. The polling firm's data does not establish AI as a direct cause of the drop, but it identified a sharp narrowing in what it calls the "engagement advantage" historically associated with employees at AI-integrated companies.

In Q3, that advantage shrank from five percentage points to two among remote employees, and from eight percentage points to three among hybrid employees, compared with prior readings.

Gallup polled a random sample of 21,648 adults aged 18 and older between Aug. 5 and Aug. 19, 2026, with a margin of sampling error of plus or minus one percentage point at a 95% confidence level. Results for remote and hybrid workers exceeded that margin of error.

The broader trend line is notable. Engagement peaked at 36% during the Covid year of 2020, dipped to 31% by 2024, and then held at that level through the first half of 2026 before the Q3 decline broke below that floor. Going back further, when Gallup first conducted the survey in 2000, engagement stood at 26% — meaning the current reading is only three points above an all-time low.

In Q3, 53% of employees were classified as "not engaged," while 18% were "actively disengaged" — meaning they are, in Gallup's framing, psychologically disconnected from their work and employer.

Other indicators reinforce the unease. In Q3, 46% of those polled said they strongly agreed they knew what was expected of them at work, down from 49% in the first half of the year. Thirty-seven percent said they strongly agreed that a supervisor or colleague cared about them, down from 40% in the first half and well below the March 2020 peak of 47%.

"The Q3 findings add to concerns about how well organizations communicate expectations and maintain employee connections across different work arrangements," Jim Harter, Gallup's chief scientist for workplace management and wellbeing practices, wrote in the survey analysis.

Harter also noted that since 2020, hybrid and fully remote employees have seen larger declines in clarity of expectations than their on-site counterparts. "These findings raise the possibility that physical distance can contribute to psychological distance between employees and their organizations, particularly as companies navigate the use of AI at work," he wrote.

Other workforce surveys published this year present a more mixed picture. The Conference Board's annual Job Satisfaction Index, released in June, reached 68.9%, the highest level in its 39-year history, though it noted gains were concentrated among higher-income workers, men, and workers confident in AI's impact on their careers. The American Psychological Association's annual workplace survey, conducted between May and June, found year-over-year declines in workers' sense that their workplace was healthy and that they felt valued, while reports of on-the-job tension and stress increased. ADP Research's Employee Motivation and Commitment Index rose in October for the second consecutive month, but remains well below its year-ago level.

Gallup cautioned that a single quarter of data cannot confirm whether a new downward trend has taken hold. Still, Harter concluded that the pattern suggests "AI adoption may be outpacing the communication and managerial support needed to make it effective" — a warning that organizations integrating AI tools may need to revisit how they manage and communicate with a workforce that is growing more detached.
