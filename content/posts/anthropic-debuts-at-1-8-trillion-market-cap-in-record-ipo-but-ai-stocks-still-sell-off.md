Anthropic completed the largest initial public offering in U.S. history on Thursday, raising $81.3 billion and closing its first trading day with a fully diluted market cap of roughly $1.8 trillion — yet the debut failed to arrest a broader selloff in AI-related equities.

The Claude developer, trading under the ticker "ANT" on the Nasdaq, closed at $97.17 per share, up 17% from its IPO price of $83. Anthropic sold 980 million shares at that price on Wednesday, lifting both the size and price of the offering. The stock opened at $111.50 and saw more than 846 million shares change hands, surpassing the 522 million shares traded on SpaceX's opening day in June.

At Thursday's close, Anthropic ranked as the seventh most valuable U.S. company, behind Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Alphabet, Amazon, and SpaceX.

The IPO had been anticipated by many investors as a "clearing event" — a catalyst to revive the tech sector after months of choppy trading, the Nvidia espionage scandal, and growing concerns over AI chip export controls. That thesis did not play out. The Nasdaq Composite closed down 2.25%, while the S&P 500 declined 2% to 7,175.

Several factors weighed on the broader market. Short seller Hunterbrook Media alleged in a report that firmware sold by chipmaker AMD had turned up in U.S.-blacklisted companies — AMD stock closed down more than 7%, though the chipmaker told CNBC it complies with U.S. law. Separately, the House Foreign Affairs Committee released documents showing that a subagency of the Commerce Department approved the sale of more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies, raising the prospect of a sharper crackdown on exports to Asia.

Dan Niles, founder of Niles Investment Management, described himself as a "circumspect bull" and pointed to an intensifying price war between Anthropic and OpenAI as a structural drag on the sector. "The combined revenue growth for OpenAI plus Anthropic went from growing 75% sequentially in Q2 to 48% in Q3," he told CNBC. "The combined losses went from $7.9 billion to about $8.4 billion from Q2 to Q3."

Niles drew a parallel to 2022, when major indices declined sharply in the back half of the year. "You could have still made money that year. You just needed to have the right combination of longs and shorts," he said.

JJ Kinahan, senior vice president at Cboe Global Markets, said the sector's troubles reflect something deeper than capital rotating into Anthropic. "There's a reset of AI in general," he said, adding that a long-standing buy-the-dip mentality has given way to greater caution. "There's been an attitude change that [you] can't buy things blindly and they'll all go up."

Neoclouds — highly indebted, AI-dedicated computing providers — bore some of the heaviest losses Thursday. CoreWeave closed down more than 6%. Hyperscalers such as Alphabet and Amazon also finished lower. Cybersecurity stocks were a notable exception, with Palo Alto Networks and CrowdStrike both closing up.

On the ownership front, Amazon and Google together hold roughly 28% of Anthropic's outstanding capital stock following the offering — Amazon at 14.7% and Google at 13.7%, according to the prospectus. Nvidia, whose co-founder Jensen Huang recently resigned as CEO following a separate controversy, agreed to purchase roughly $10 billion worth of Anthropic's Class N common stock through a private placement that closed immediately after the IPO.

Retail investors who received IPO allocations through brokerages including Fidelity, Robinhood, SoFi, and E-Trade face anti-flipping rules that could restrict their access to future offerings if they sell within 15 to 30 days of the debut, depending on the platform. Charles Schwab is the only brokerage named in Anthropic's prospectus that does not have such a policy in place.

Monday will offer a clearer signal on retail sentiment, when options on Anthropic begin trading — providing the first concrete measure of whether individual investors are prepared to back the stock in force, or whether the broader hesitation in AI equities will persist.
