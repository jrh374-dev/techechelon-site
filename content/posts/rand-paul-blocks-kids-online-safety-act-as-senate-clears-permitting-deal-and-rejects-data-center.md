The U.S. Senate produced a flurry of competing tech and energy policy outcomes on Wednesday, as Sen. Rand Paul (R-Ky.) blocked a unanimous consent push for the Kids Online Safety Act, bipartisan lawmakers unveiled a permitting reform agreement, and Senate Democrats killed a House-passed bill targeting data center electricity costs.

Paul objected to an effort led by Sens. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) and Marsha Blackburn (R-Tenn.) to pass the Kids Online Safety Act by unanimous consent. KOSA, as the bill is known, is designed to hold social media companies accountable for alleged harms they cause to minors and has been a long-sought legislative priority for its sponsors.

A single senator can block unanimous consent proceedings, and Paul's objection was enough to halt the push entirely. The move stalls a bill that has drawn sustained bipartisan attention in recent sessions of Congress.

On a separate track, a group of Democratic and Republican senators announced a long-sought deal on permitting reform, according to officials familiar with the agreement. The legislation is intended to accelerate construction of energy and infrastructure projects across the country while also reining in costs associated with data centers.

The bipartisan nature of the permitting deal is notable given the difficulty such agreements have historically faced in clearing both chambers. Permitting reform has been a recurring sticking point between the two parties, with disagreements over environmental review timelines and project approval processes frequently stalling negotiations.

Meanwhile, Senate Democrats blocked the Ratepayer Protection Act, a bill that had passed the House with significant bipartisan support and sought to address the rising electricity costs tied to data center operations. Democrats characterized the legislation as ineffective and "toothless," and they denied it the 60 votes required to advance in the Senate.

The fate of the Ratepayer Protection Act underscores the disconnect that can emerge between House and Senate priorities even within the same party. The bill's House passage had signaled broad appetite for legislating around data center energy consumption, a concern that has grown alongside the rapid expansion of artificial intelligence infrastructure.

The trio of actions on a single legislative day reflects the Senate's complicated relationship with tech-adjacent policy. Energy permitting, children's online safety, and data center electricity costs all occupy different corners of the policy landscape, but each touches on how lawmakers are grappling with the infrastructure demands and social consequences of an increasingly digital economy.

KOSA's advocates are expected to continue seeking a path forward, though the unanimous consent route is now closed without Paul's cooperation. With the permitting deal now in text form, the next test will be whether leadership can bring it to the floor and hold together its bipartisan coalition through a full vote.
