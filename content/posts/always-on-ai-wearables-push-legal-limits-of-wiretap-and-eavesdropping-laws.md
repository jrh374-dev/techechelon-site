A new generation of AI-powered wearables from Apple, Amazon, Meta, and a range of startups is raising largely unsettled legal questions about privacy and wiretapping — with experts warning that in some states, the legal exposure may fall on the person wearing the device, not the company that made it.

The latest additions to the market arrived Thursday with Amazon's Bee Frontier, a wearable that can be worn on a wrist or clipped to clothing, and the Bee Eclipse, a card-shaped device that attaches to a smartphone. Both products are designed to record, transcribe, and analyze a user's daily conversations.

Apple's Apple Watch Series 12, unveiled in September, introduced a feature called Siri Recap, which "ambiently" takes notes throughout the day and generates summaries of conversations. A companion feature called Live Rewind can produce a text transcription of the previous 15 seconds of audio. Meta's smart glasses, meanwhile, can capture photos, video, and voice notes. Startups including Plaud, Pocket, and Friend have introduced similar card-sized or pendant-based note-taking devices.

What separates this wave of gadgets from earlier voice assistants is their continuous operation. Unlike smart speakers that activated upon hearing a "wake word," some of the newer devices can listen, interpret speech, and extract meaning without retaining an audio file — a technical distinction that may not resolve the legal question of whether an unlawful interception has occurred.

"With new technology like this, my statement is always to embrace with caution," said Karen Panetta, dean of graduate education at Tufts' School of Engineering and a fellow at the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers. "If I'm giving permission for myself to be recorded, or for everything I look at to be recorded, that doesn't mean everybody else around you has given that consent."

Apple has said Siri Recap does not store audio, send recordings to its servers, or involve human review. Amazon has said it does not store audio from Bee devices, and that conversations are processed in a secure environment "where no one, including Amazon, can access your data."

Colin Zick, a privacy and data security attorney at Foley Hoag, said those assurances may not resolve the underlying legal exposure. Many state wiretap and eavesdropping statutes are written around whether a private communication was intercepted or its contents were acquired — not simply whether a conventional audio file was created or stored.

Massachusetts, for example, defines an intercepting device to include equipment capable of "transmitting, receiving, amplifying, or recording" a communication, and defines the "contents" of a communication to include its substance and meaning. In Zick's view, a device that continuously receives speech and produces a written summary could potentially fall within that language even without retaining the underlying audio.

Crucially, Zick noted that the legal risk in such states may rest with the wearer rather than the manufacturer. "A non-wearer did not buy the watch, agree to Apple's terms, or choose to have their conversation summarized in the app," Zick said.

That dynamic is already playing out in everyday settings. Chris Thomas, a 43-year-old Pittsburgh-based business owner and livestreamer, wears Meta's camera-equipped glasses, a Fieldy note-taking pendant, and an Amazon Bee wristband simultaneously. He said an encounter at a local bar earlier this week illustrated how bystanders are responding. "He was like, 'I don't feel comfortable,'" Thomas said of another patron who noticed his Meta glasses. "I don't turn it off, I take it off completely because people don't really believe it's off."

The legal landscape remains largely untested. Amazon, Google, and other makers of earlier voice-activated smart speakers faced privacy lawsuits and regulatory fines over related concerns, but those cases did not have to address whether continuous speech processing — without audio retention — constitutes interception under state law.

As the installed base of always-on AI wearables grows, that question is likely to reach the courts, with state-level wiretap statutes varying widely in how they could apply to technology their authors never anticipated.
