More than 43% of U.S. workers report using artificial intelligence on the job, according to a March study from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis — but experts say many professionals are struggling to draw the line between AI that helps and AI that harms.

The concern, at its core, is about what happens when workers stop thinking for themselves.

Sandra Matz, a professor at Columbia Business School, said her research points to two direct consequences of over-reliance on AI tools: stagnation and mediocrity. "When employees use it to outsource critical thinking or creative reasoning, there's a real risk of gradually falling behind and becoming complacent," Matz said.

Part of the problem, she explained, is structural. Many AI tools are designed to produce the most obvious and safe responses, pushing outputs toward uniformity. "In short, we become just like everyone else," she said.

Americus Reed II, a professor of marketing at the Wharton School of Business, said colleagues in his field describe this pattern as "cognitive surrender." Reed drew a distinction between AI that removes friction and AI that replaces judgment. "It should remove friction, not replace judgment," he said. "The goal is to spend less time processing information and more time creating meaning."

Fred Oswald, an industrial-organizational psychologist and former member of the National Artificial Intelligence Advisory Committee, offered a broader framing of the challenge. "There is a Wild West of AI use that's worth understanding and exploring, so that employees and organizations can manage risks and further benefit," Oswald said.

Younger workers face a specific set of risks, according to Dorothy Leidner, a business and ethics professor at the McIntire School of Commerce at the University of Virginia. Without deep existing expertise, early-career professionals may lean on AI as a thought generator rather than a thought enhancer — undermining the learning process that builds that expertise in the first place. "The risk is higher for younger workers who do not have a deep expertise to help them use AI as a thought enhancer rather than a thought generator," Leidner said.

The interpersonal dimension of AI use at work also drew scrutiny. Matz cautioned that using AI to draft emails or Slack messages to colleagues will "gradually erode trust." Informal workplace communication depends on spontaneity and imperfection, she argued. "The moment we suspect that a message has been outsourced to AI, we might start to question whether our colleagues care enough to write to us themselves," she said.

Leidner extended the concern to so-called "digital twins" — AI systems that simulate a person's communication style. "If we get to the point where AI is generating the emails and AI is responding to the emails, then there is no actual communication taking place," she said.

Eva Selenko, a work psychology professor at Loughborough University in England, said her primary concern centers on workers who rely on AI for tasks central to their professional identity. "Witnessing a tool outperforming you on a central task of your expertise can be a status threat," Selenko said. "If these opportunities are taken away, your self-concept gets a bit more shaky."

Reed acknowledged that such experiences may be unavoidable as the technology improves, but said the answer is not to disengage from AI entirely. "Workers must remember that adopting AI is not the same as outsourcing your identity," he said. "Technology changes quickly, but the uniquely human skills — judgment, creativity, curiosity and relationships — become even more valuable."

Matz agreed that AI remains a legitimate productivity tool when applied to automating mundane tasks or serving as a brainstorming partner. The line, she and others say, is drawn at the point where the tool begins substituting for the reasoning that defines a worker's value — a line that, as adoption accelerates, will require active organizational attention to maintain.
