Telecom stocks suffered sharp declines on Friday after SpaceX announced an agreement to acquire a nationwide spectrum portfolio, signaling Elon Musk's ambition to expand Starlink's satellite internet service into direct competition with established U.S. mobile carriers.

Verizon closed down 8.75% on October 9, its steepest single-day drop since July 2002. T-Mobile fell 13.27%, marking its worst day since 2013, while AT&T shed 9.81%, its largest single-session loss since 2000. SpaceX shares rose approximately 1% on the day.

The deal centers on the acquisition of spectrum from Grain Management. SpaceX described the transaction as a "license portfolio of up to 14 megahertz of paired spectrum in the 800 MHz band" in a statement issued Thursday.

Federal Communications Commission Chair Brendan Carr, whose agency will oversee regulatory approval of the deal, offered a measured endorsement of the competitive shift. "We're probably going to bring over $100 billion of spectrum into the market over the next two years, so we're seeing a lot of competition," Carr told CNBC's "Squawk on the Street" on Friday.

"There's going to be moves in the market. People are going to try to out-compete, out-innovate, and I think that's great. We're going to see how this plays out. It's not for us ultimately to pick winners and losers," he added.

Analysts were divided on the immediacy of the threat to incumbents. Evercore ISI wrote that SpaceX "now has the outline of a real network" alongside its existing satellite capabilities — a notable shift in how the rocket maker's broadband ambitions are being assessed.

JPMorgan took a more cautious view of near-term disruption. The firm wrote that the acquisition made Starlink Mobile's long-term opportunity "more credible," but that analysts "continue to see limited near-term risk to U.S. wireless incumbents given the time, infrastructure and capital required to build a competitive terrestrial network."

The selloff reflects how seriously investors are pricing competitive pressure even before SpaceX has demonstrated an operational terrestrial network, underscoring the degree to which Musk's track record in satellite broadband has altered the market's calculus on wireless competition.

The spectrum acquisition still requires FCC approval, and the timeline for SpaceX to build out a network capable of challenging Verizon, T-Mobile, or AT&T at scale remains undefined. What the deal does establish is that Starlink Mobile now holds the foundational licensing assets — 800 MHz band spectrum, known for its strong building penetration and wide coverage range — that any serious domestic wireless entrant would need.

For the three incumbents, the question is no longer whether SpaceX will attempt to compete in terrestrial mobile, but how quickly it can convert spectrum rights and satellite infrastructure into a commercially viable service.
