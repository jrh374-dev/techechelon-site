Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan announced Wednesday that the company has secured its first outside customer for its 18A chip manufacturing process, a milestone the chipmaker has pursued for five years as it works to compete with Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. in the contract chip business.

Tan made the disclosure at the TomorrowX Summit in Austin, Texas, without naming the new client. "And then 18A, I'm involved in production and we just signed the first customer," he said.

Intel's technology chief Pushkar Ranade clarified on X that the external customer signed up specifically for the 18A-P manufacturing process node, which entered risk production in June.

The announcement carries added weight given Intel's recent financial difficulties. The company's stock has shed more than half its value since the end of September, with the steepest drop coming in late October after Intel said quarterly revenue would fall at least $1 billion short of analyst estimates, citing a PC sales slump and supply chain challenges.

For years, Intel manufactured chips almost exclusively for its own products, leaving it without the marquee foundry clients that anchor TSMC's business model. The 18A process has been central to Tan's pitch that Intel can finally serve outside customers at a competitive level.

Ben Bajarin, an analyst at Creative Strategies, called the development "a huge win for foundry." He pointed to Nvidia, Apple, and the major cloud hyperscalers as the most likely candidates for the unnamed customer, noting that Amazon, Microsoft, and Google have struggled to secure sufficient capacity at TSMC for their custom processor ambitions.

Reports published in May indicated that Apple and Intel had been closing in on a deal to use Intel's foundry for some Apple device chips, though no agreement was confirmed at the time.

Tan also announced Wednesday that Microsoft has become the first publicly named customer for Intel's most advanced chip-packaging technology, known as EMIB — a separate but complementary milestone for the foundry operation.

Intel's foundry push has attracted significant institutional backing. The U.S. government took a 10% stake in the company in August 2025, and Nvidia followed with a $5 billion investment, both moves driven by interest in expanding domestic chip manufacturing capacity that could reduce near-total dependence on Taiwan.

Tan said in late October that he projects the global market for server processors could grow as much as five times over the next five years, reaching roughly $200 billion — though he acknowledged Intel currently cannot meet existing customer demand.

Whether the first 18A-P customer signals a broader commercial revival for Intel's foundry unit will depend in large part on yield improvements, production ramp timelines, and the company's ability to close the gap with TSMC on advanced process nodes — challenges that have derailed earlier recovery efforts.
