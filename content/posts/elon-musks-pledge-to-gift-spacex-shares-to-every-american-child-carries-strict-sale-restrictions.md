Elon Musk announced Monday that he intends to give every child in America a share of SpaceX next year, a pledge that would move through the Trump Account program — and come with significant restrictions that limit when beneficiaries can sell.

Musk posted the announcement on X, appearing to suggest the shares would be deposited into Trump Accounts, the federally backed children's investment accounts also known as 530A accounts. In the same post, he thanked Brad Gerstner, a key architect of the program, for "helping enable this."

The gift, if executed, would be subject to U.S. Department of the Treasury guidelines that the agency rolled out in recent weeks for stock donations to Trump Accounts. Under those temporary regulations — which remain subject to revision after public comment — families cannot refuse stock gifted to Trump Accounts and generally cannot sell it until the earlier of five years or the end of the calendar year in which the beneficiary turns 17.

Early sales are permitted only in narrow circumstances, such as certain corporate transitions or when a stock is formally delisted from a national securities exchange. Financial or legal distress at the company does not qualify as grounds for an early sale.

"Giving families more choices and more ability to hold the assets that they think are best for them and their kids' future ... would be a good thing," said Adam Michel, director of tax policy studies at the Cato Institute, a libertarian think tank. "But this is not really that, because you have no choice in the asset that is being deposited into your child's account, and then you also have no ability to sell it for the five-year holding period."

Lawyers and philanthropic experts have noted that the Treasury's framework gives donors an unusual degree of influence over children's investments. In conventional charitable giving, donors may express preferences about how gifts are managed, but the receiving organization or trustee typically retains final authority.

"It's the donor that's dictating the terms of the gift," said Carolyn Schenck, an attorney and former national fraud counsel at the IRS, who left the agency in 2025 for law firm Caplin & Drysdale.

The temporary regulations also require that if any gifted stock is sold too early, the trustee must repurchase the same number and class of shares "as soon as practicable." Schenck said that provision increases beneficiaries' risk if the share price rises before the trustee can complete the repurchase — a cost that would ultimately fall on the account holder.

"If the fiduciary makes an investment mistake, then the focus is typically restoring value to the beneficiary. But here, the focus seems to be restoring the specific security itself," Schenck said.

The regulations themselves cite donor interests as one rationale for the holding period. According to the text, "Donors are attracted by the hope that a stock contribution with a five-year holding period may lead recipients and their families to feel that they have a stake in the fate of the corporation."

Jane Ditelberg, chief tax strategist at Northern Trust, said the structure also insulates donors and their companies from the risk that a mass influx of new shareholders might immediately sell their positions and depress the share price. "Certainly, for people who have highly concentrated wealth in a company, and the assets are sitting in their own personal pockets, this makes it a lot easier to give," she said.

Musk would not be the first SpaceX insider to make such a pledge. SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell announced earlier this summer that she would gift approximately 2 million SpaceX shares through the Trump Account program. That distribution has not yet taken place.

According to the Treasury, roughly 70 million Trump Accounts have been created, including more than 60 million through a recent auto-enrollment effort. The accounts are available to all U.S. citizen children under age 18 who hold valid Social Security numbers. Representatives for Musk, Gerstner, and the Treasury Department did not respond to requests for comment on the proposed gift.
