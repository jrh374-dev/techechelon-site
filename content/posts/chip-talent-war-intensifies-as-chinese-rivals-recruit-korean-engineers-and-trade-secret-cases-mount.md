A former Samsung Electronics engineer who left to join Chinese memory chipmaker ChangXin Memory Technologies was sentenced by the Seoul High Court on Oct. 22 to five years in prison for leaking Samsung's proprietary DRAM technology — a ruling that underscores the deepening legal and competitive friction at the heart of the global semiconductor industry.

The sentence was reduced from seven years handed down at the engineer's first trial in April. The individual had joined CXMT in 2016, according to court records.

The case is not isolated. In 2023, a South Korean court granted Samsung's request to block a former DRAM researcher from joining U.S. rival Micron, ruling that a two-year non-compete agreement was not excessive and was necessary to protect Samsung's technology and business information.

Chinese semiconductor companies have been aggressively recruiting experienced engineers from established rivals, offering high salaries, stock options, housing allowances, and family support, according to Jeongku Choi, a senior analyst at Counterpoint Research. That expertise has helped Chinese chipmakers overcome technical bottlenecks, particularly as U.S. export restrictions have limited China's access to advanced chipmaking equipment, Choi said.

CXMT, once considered unlikely to close the gap with leading memory producers, is now projected to hold a 10% share of global DRAM revenue in the second quarter of 2026, according to Counterpoint's Memory Tracker. Chinese manufacturers broadly are now viewed as "meaningful competitors," Choi said.

A decade ago, Samsung's primary concern about departing engineers centered on rivals such as SK Hynix and Micron, according to a former Samsung employee who asked not to be identified because the information was private. Chinese chipmakers have since become a central focus of that concern.

Samsung has used non-compete agreements restricting departures to rival firms for two years, with senior employees holding valuable technical expertise likely facing closer scrutiny. The company has also monitored where certain departing engineers went, in part to signal consequences to remaining staff. "It wasn't just about one employee leaving. The company wanted to send a message to those who stayed that moving to a competitor wouldn't be easy," the former employee said.

Established chipmakers are responding by strengthening legal protections and internal security measures while adjusting compensation packages to retain key engineers, Choi said. South Korea has also designated certain semiconductor manufacturing technologies as "National Core Technologies," raising the penalties for unauthorized leaks, she added.

The legal environment varies significantly across the region. In Taiwan — home to TSMC, the world's largest contract chipmaker — post-employment non-compete agreements are limited to two years, and employers must compensate former employees at least half their average monthly salary during the restricted period, according to Jamie J. Yang, a partner specializing in antitrust and intellectual property at Innovatus Law.

Yang noted that the complexity of semiconductor manufacturing limits how much any single engineer can transfer to a competitor. "There is just very little chance that one person... will know enough to replicate that line in any of the other companies," she said.

In Singapore, Zechariah Chan, a partner at law firm Lee & Lee, said the challenge is compounded by the intangible nature of semiconductor know-how. Proving misappropriation is difficult because outsiders typically see only a finished product, not the manufacturing techniques behind it. "There is no bright line between an engineer's expertise and proprietary information," Chan said. "You cannot 'take' the information back after the engineer has become aware of it."

Choi expects the legal dimensions of the talent competition to broaden considerably as Chinese memory producers look beyond their domestic market. "I believe that this management of trade secrets will expand its scope even further to IP and legal areas once Chinese memory companies move beyond the Chinese market and begin to expand their exports," she said.

Trade secret litigation across the semiconductor industry is expected to rise as firms seek to protect their technological edge, Yang added — signaling that the courtroom may become as contested a battleground as the fabrication floor itself.
