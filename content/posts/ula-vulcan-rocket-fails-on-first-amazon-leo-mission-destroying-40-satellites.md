United Launch Alliance suffered what is reportedly its first mission failure in its 20-year history on Saturday, losing 40 Amazon Leo broadband satellites when its Vulcan rocket malfunctioned roughly ten minutes after liftoff from Space Launch Complex 41.

"Based on data received by our Launch Director, we confirm that the Vulcan vehicle launched from Space Launch Complex 41 suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit," ULA said in a statement posted on X.

The company said the cause of the failure remained unclear and pledged transparency as its investigation continues.

The failed mission, designated LV-01, was the first of 38 Vulcan flights Amazon had purchased from ULA to build out its Leo constellation. Leo is Amazon's direct competitor to SpaceX's Starlink, though it has struggled to establish a meaningful foothold, having placed only a small fraction of the satellites needed to mount a credible challenge to Starlink into orbit.

Saturday's loss sets that effort back further, wiping out 40 spacecraft in a single event before they could reach their intended altitude.

Amazon moved quickly to signal continued confidence in its launch partner. "There is risk with any launch, and that's especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01," the company said in a statement posted on X. "ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they'll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan."

The Vulcan rocket has been ULA's next-generation launch vehicle, designed to eventually replace the company's Atlas V and Delta IV heavy-lift rockets. A failure on the first contracted Amazon mission adds pressure to a program that has already faced delays and scrutiny as competition in the commercial launch market has intensified.

The loss arrives at a particularly difficult moment for Amazon's space ambitions. Leo has been racing to close an enormous gap with Starlink, which operates thousands of satellites and serves millions of customers globally. With 38 contracted Vulcan flights still on the books, the program's long-term trajectory now depends heavily on ULA's ability to identify the anomaly's cause and certify the vehicle for future flights.

ULA's investigation will draw close attention from both Amazon and the broader commercial space sector, as the outcome will shape the near-term launch cadence of one of Starlink's most well-resourced rivals.
