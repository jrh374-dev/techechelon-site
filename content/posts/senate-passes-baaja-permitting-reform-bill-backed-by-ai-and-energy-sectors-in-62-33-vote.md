The Senate on Thursday passed a sweeping bill to overhaul the nation's environmental permitting laws and electrical transmission regulations, clearing a critical legislative hurdle for the energy and technology industries that have long pushed for the changes.

Senators voted 62-33 to approve the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026, known as BAAJA, which now heads to the House for a vote before it can reach President Donald Trump's desk.

The bill's passage exposed a rift inside the Democratic Party just weeks after it secured enough seats in the midterm elections to hold majorities in both chambers of Congress come January. More Democrats voted against the measure than voted for it, though more than a dozen — including Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer of New York — crossed over to push it through over objections from climate groups and Native American tribes.

"No big, bipartisan bill is going to be perfect for everyone, but we have a real opportunity to make electricity more affordable for families. Working people can't afford to wait another few years for us to get this done," Sen. Martin Heinrich, D-N.M., the incoming chair of the Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee and a lead author of the bill, said in a statement.

Heinrich brokered the agreement alongside Sen. Mike Lee, R-Utah, Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse, D-R.I., and Sen. Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va. — the top Democrat and Republican on the Senate Environment and Public Works Committee.

Sen. Ruben Gallego, D-Ariz., who supported the measure, told reporters Thursday before the vote that it would be "legislation that's really going to make the biggest difference for the next 100 years, especially bringing down carbon emissions once this president is gone."

The bill is projected to lower energy costs for consumers while streamlining permitting processes, changes that renewables, fossil fuel producers, and technology companies have all sought. Clean energy groups and oil and gas associations alike welcomed the Senate outcome.

The American Clean Power Association called it "a clean energy bill that will modernize America's energy systems and lower consumer prices." The American Petroleum Institute described it as "a major breakthrough on historic permitting reforms that would deliver more affordable energy for consumers, cut costly delays, and build much-needed critical infrastructure."

According to Rhodium Group, an economic research firm, the bill could allow between 17 and 249 gigawatts of additional clean energy to be built by 2040. The firm also estimates greenhouse gas emissions would be reduced by 5 to 148 million metric tons annually in 2040, while natural gas exports would rise 8% to 15% beyond a 76% to 145% increase already projected under current policy.

A central component of the legislation involves electricity transmission rules — an area that has bottlenecked new solar and wind projects unable to deliver power to the grid. The bill also includes provisions aimed at shielding already-permitted wind and solar projects from cancellation.

"A lot of Democrats are involved and supportive in this deal because expanding grid capacity would integrate a lot of clean energy," said Rob Gramlich, founder and president of Grid Strategies, an energy consulting group. "Republicans are maybe less interested in that benefit, but they do care about economic growth and serving new large loads."

The tech industry stands to benefit as well, as power-hungry data centers undergirding artificial intelligence demand more electricity. The bill includes a provision requiring hyperscalers to pay for grid upgrades, though opponents warned the measure could hand data center operators broad authority over public resources.

"I'm not going to sit around and let these data centers scar our treasures," Sen. Ron Wyden, D-Ore., told reporters Thursday, referencing his amendment — which failed — that would have barred data centers from being built on public lands.

The bill's path to the House is expected to test the same Democratic fault lines that surfaced in the Senate, with affordability-focused moderates squaring off against members aligned with environmental advocacy groups ahead of the new Congress taking power in January.
