Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei pushed back this week against claims that his warnings about artificial intelligence have fueled a public backlash against the technology, arguing instead that the skepticism stems from a decades-long erosion of trust in corporations and institutions.

Amodei's remarks, [first reported by TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/16/anthropic-ceo-says-ai-backlash-is-fundamentally-a-crisis-of-trust), came in direct response to investor Gavin Baker, who argued on the All-In podcast and on the social platform X that Amodei's risk-focused messaging has helped stoke resistance to AI in the United States, particularly opposition to data centers.

Baker contended that Amodei has "lost the argument" when it comes to AI regulation and, given that "he is about to be the CEO of one of the most important companies in the world," suggested he "should make an effort to be a more positive advocate for his own industry."

Amodei rejected the framing. He said his writing has been "about equally balanced between risks and benefits," pointing to his essay "Machines of Loving Grace" as evidence that he has actively tried to articulate an inspiring vision of how AI could improve the world — a vision he felt the broader industry was failing to communicate.

Still, Amodei acknowledged the scale of the problem. "The public has a negative view of AI," he said, calling it "a big problem." He disputed, however, that the cause lay primarily with him or any other AI executive who has spoken openly about risks.

"I think it is fundamentally a crisis of trust," Amodei said. "I think that ordinary people don't trust companies, governments, or the tech industry and always suspect that we are cooking up some new way to screw them over."

In his telling, the AI backlash is "just the latest iteration" of a crisis that has been building for decades, not something manufactured by cautious messaging from AI leaders.

On the question of where AI companies have genuinely fallen short, Amodei was direct. "I think by far the most accurate criticism of AI companies including Anthropic is that we haven't yet delivered on our big promises to benefit the world," he said. "That is totally on us, and I think it's the criticism you should be making, instead of all this stuff about messaging and marketing."

Amodei also addressed the regulation debate, pushing back on what he described as a false choice between deploying AI freely without constraints or allowing regulation to concentrate power among a handful of large companies.

"I know that there's a sort of Silicon Valley shorthand where regulation = regulatory capture = concentration of power, but I've always found this to be an overly simplified picture of the world," he said. He noted that many people outside that circle view regulation as a mechanism to constrain corporate power rather than entrench it.

He added that Anthropic has tried to design its policy proposals to "disadvantage — slow down — frontier AI companies while advantaging smaller competitors," a position he said reflects the company's deliberate approach to policy work.

Anthropic has previously advocated for certain regulatory measures, including a California bill that would impose transparency requirements on large AI companies, a stance that has itself drawn criticism from parts of the industry.

The exchange highlights a widening debate inside the AI sector over how executives should communicate about their technology's risks, with companies like Anthropic navigating pressure from investors, regulators, and a skeptical public simultaneously. How that trust gap is bridged — whether through policy, delivery on promised benefits, or both — is likely to shape the regulatory environment for AI development in the months ahead.
