OpenAI on Friday closed a $110 billion funding round led by SoftBank's Vision Fund 2 and Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, with participation from Microsoft, Khosla Ventures, and Tiger Global. The round values the company at $500 billion — more than the market capitalization of all but seven public companies — and gives the firm capital to commit to a five-year buildout of compute infrastructure that internal documents describe as "unprecedented in any prior industry."

The figures sound abstract. They are not. By 2028, the buildout will require approximately **47 gigawatts** of new electricity generation: enough to power the entire state of New York at peak summer demand, and roughly 3 percent of total U.S. generation capacity.

## Power as the new bottleneck

For two decades, hyperscale computing was a problem that engineers solved with money. The new constraint is physical: the U.S. power grid was not designed for loads of this scale, and the permitting timeline for new generation runs five to seven years.

That timeline does not bend. Regulators can prioritize, but they cannot compress a multi-year environmental review into the 18-month window OpenAI's roadmap assumes. The math creates a forcing function for federal action.

SoftBank's commitment alone is reported to be **$40 billion**, the firm's largest single check ever written. The structure of the deal includes a backstop provision that requires participating power utilities in three states to fast-track interconnection studies for OpenAI-affiliated data center sites — a structural innovation that surprised even people close to the negotiation.

Microsoft's participation is smaller than its prior commitments but strategically significant. People familiar with the talks say the company pushed for and received a renewed compute-purchase guarantee that runs through 2031, locking in a relationship that some analysts had begun to question.

## Washington watches, but quietly

The federal Council on AI finds itself with little authority over what is, in functional terms, an energy infrastructure rollout. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's jurisdiction is limited; the Department of Energy's role is advisory. Two senators are drafting legislation, but staff concede the timeline is unlikely to matter for the first phase of construction.

The next AI policy fight will not be about model safety or content moderation. It will be about who pays for the grid upgrades that compute requires, and how fast permitting can move. Watch the next round of FERC nominees, and watch the state public service commissions in Texas, Virginia, and Arizona. That is where this decision actually gets made.
