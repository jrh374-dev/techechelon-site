Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh convened a conference in San Francisco on Monday dedicated to examining what artificial intelligence could mean for productivity, employment, and ultimately interest rates — signaling that the central bank is beginning to formally grapple with AI's economic consequences.

"We are at a hinge point in the U.S. economy," Warsh said at the event, which was organized around his AI task force. He stopped short of drawing direct conclusions about monetary policy, leaving much of the substantive speculation to the outside experts he has assembled.

The conference is part of a broader effort by Warsh to bring outside voices into a central bank he has long criticized as insular. Rather than relying exclusively on the Fed's internal staff of Ph.D. economists, Warsh has built five separate task forces — one of them devoted to AI's effects on productivity and jobs — drawing expertise from beyond the institution.

One of the three official members of the AI task force, venture capitalist Marc Andreessen, spoke at Monday's event. A second member, Stanford economist Chad Jones, could not participate because he is on leave and working at Anthropic, which is currently observing a blackout period ahead of its planned initial public offering. The third member, Xbox CEO Asha Sharma, did not attend.

Andreessen expressed optimism about AI's technological promise but argued that government regulation would blunt its economic effects. Heavy regulation in sectors such as healthcare and education means the technology cannot readily displace workers in those fields, he said.

"To the extent our economy is a free-market economy, then maybe you could see the, let's say, extremely rapid displacement scenarios people are talking about," Andreessen said. "But we've mostly outlawed the kind of job transition that … both the utopians and the doomers talk about."

Warsh returned repeatedly to the analogy of the so-called China shock — the wave of job losses that followed China's entry into the World Trade Organization in 2001. One widely cited estimate attributed the loss of a million manufacturing jobs, and 2.4 million jobs overall, to that period. Warsh raised the possibility that AI could deliver a similar shock to service workers, potentially lowering prices for work such as legal or administrative services, while displacing the people who perform them.

"If in some ways over the last generation or generation and a half, the China shock, for lack of a better word, lowered prices of goods in the United States, the way that I heard you talking about the philosophy of this new technology is it might do something for services," Warsh said at the event.

Panelists largely agreed that AI-driven disinflation in the services sector was possible over the medium term — an outcome that could eventually push the Fed toward lower interest rates. But Warsh offered no immediate conclusions, and the distance between a task force discussion and a change in monetary policy remains considerable.

The conference unfolded against a charged backdrop. AI lab Anthropic is planning to price its IPO shares on Wednesday, with a potential valuation of $1.4 trillion and a fundraise of as much as $64 billion. Meanwhile, the November 3 midterm elections saw voters pass a series of state-level measures aimed at slowing construction of data centers.

Former Commerce Secretary and Rhode Island Governor Gina Raimondo, one of the few prominent Democrats to push back publicly on that trend, called the results "a very troubling sign." Stopping AI "would be very bad for America," she said.

Warsh moderated three of the four conference sessions. The task force members are expected to compile a report for Warsh within the next few months, after which the Fed itself would need to debate the findings and determine whether they warrant any shift in policy.
