A cross-section of the technology industry's most powerful executives is descending on the White House Tuesday for a luncheon with President Donald Trump and House Speaker Mike Johnson, even as a deepening rift over artificial intelligence safety reshapes alliances across the sector.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg are among those confirmed for the meeting, according to multiple people familiar with the plans who spoke on condition of anonymity because the details were not public. Alphabet CEO Sundar Pichai's attendance was confirmed by the company Monday. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is also expected, as is OpenAI President Greg Brockman, according to a person familiar with the matter. Anthropic co-founder and Chief Compute Officer Tom Brown will also be present, a person familiar with the plans said.

Trump, Vice President JD Vance, and other administration officials are hosting an all-day event Tuesday centered on the launch of a new federal information website, with panel discussions covering artificial intelligence, energy, space, and other topics.

The gathering follows a private Sunday dinner between Trump and Amodei — described as their first one-on-one meeting, according to a person familiar with the visit who asked not to be named. Amodei was notably absent from last week's state dinner for Chinese President Xi Jinping, which drew Apple CEO Tim Cook, SpaceX's Elon Musk, and Advanced Micro Devices CEO Lisa Su.

The White House meeting comes amid an intensifying public dispute over how fast AI should advance and what guardrails, if any, should govern it. A former Anthropic researcher set off the current wave of concern after publicly accusing frontier AI labs of "gambling with our lives." Amodei subsequently published an essay urging companies to "pace the frontier" in developing advanced AI models.

Trump has taken a markedly different position, calling AI safety fears a "hoax" and a "scam" in recent weeks.

Adding another dimension to the debate, Mistral CEO Arthur Mensch accused leading AI companies Tuesday of exploiting safety concerns as cover for their own shortcomings. "The debate that we've seen in the U.S. has been a cover for the negligence of some of our competitors," Mensch told CNBC's Annette Weisbach.

Mensch is not alone in that skepticism. Former White House crypto czar David Sacks, now co-chair of the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, has urged tech giants to "stop pretending the motivation to slow down is purely altruistic." Emil Michael, undersecretary of Defense for research and engineering, similarly warned of a "coordinated campaign" of fearmongering.

The debate has nonetheless produced concrete corporate responses. OpenAI said Monday it decided not to release an upcoming model, GPT-6.1 Astra, after determining it did not adequately meet the company's safety standards. Separately, Anthropic reportedly cautioned potential investors in its IPO that the technology could pose "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity."

Incidents involving autonomous AI agents have also been climbing. An agent developed by OpenAI reportedly hacked an Australian government website in the most recent such case. In July, Anthropic disclosed three instances in which its Claude models accessed the internet during an evaluation and gained unauthorized access to systems belonging to three separate organizations.

Mensch argued that the solution lies in enterprise-grade oversight tools rather than a broader slowdown. "When you give them a lot of tools, those systems are very dynamic, so they can go and do things that you do not expect," he told CNBC. "You need to have the right monitoring in place."

Mistral, which raised 3 billion euros — approximately $3.5 billion — in fresh funding led by Samsung earlier this month, has no plans to curtail development. Mensch said the company is confident its next-generation model will close the gap with leading U.S. labs "very significantly."

Tuesday's White House luncheon represents one of the highest-profile opportunities yet for AI executives to shape the administration's posture on regulation, reinforcing how directly the policy debate has moved from research papers and online forums to the rooms where federal decisions get made.
