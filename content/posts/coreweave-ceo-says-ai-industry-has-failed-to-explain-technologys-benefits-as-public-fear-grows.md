CoreWeave CEO Mike Intrator acknowledged Wednesday that artificial intelligence companies have not adequately communicated the benefits of their technology to the public, remarks that landed as opposition to data center construction continues to intensify across communities nationwide.

Intrator made the comments during the Goldman Sachs Communcacopia + Technology Conference, an annual gathering that brings together leaders from the largest technology and media companies.

"I don't think we have done a particularly good job of talking about the benefits of what the data centers allow the AI companies to be able to deliver to people, to governments, to decision making," Intrator told CNBC's David Faber in comments that aired Wednesday.

Intrator argued that the core of public resistance is not hostility toward data centers specifically, but anxiety about the pace of technological transformation. "It is really about: Hey, you know the world is changing, and it's changing very quickly, and that's going to have impacts on myself. It's going to have impacts on my children, and what is that going to look like? And that is frightening," he said.

The remarks arrived alongside a separate flashpoint in the AI debate. Jacob Coxon, a researcher who worked at both Anthropic and its chief competitor OpenAI, publicly resigned Tuesday with a warning that the technology "could kill us all by the end of the decade."

Coxon accused the companies of "gambling with our lives" in the push toward AI superintelligence. "Do not underestimate the power of this technology. These will soon be superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources. We have all witnessed the progress in each of these domains, and progress is not slowing," Coxon wrote in a post on X announcing his departure.

Separately, a bipartisan coalition of organizations is pressing the Trump administration to publicly disclose its framework for testing government AI systems before wider deployment. "The government has a fundamental responsibility to share the parameters of the process for reviewing frontier AI models prior to wider deployment," the coalition stated, citing transparency concerns over the administration's closed-door approach.

The confluence of events — an industry CEO conceding a communications failure, a high-profile researcher exit framed as a safety warning, and a cross-partisan push for regulatory disclosure — reflects deepening friction between the pace of AI deployment and public and institutional trust in the sector.

Also surfacing at the Goldman Sachs conference Wednesday were concerns about AI's downstream effects on commodity markets. Copper demand is rising in part because of the power infrastructure required to build and operate AI data centers, while global copper mine production fell 1.1% in the first half of 2026, according to data from the International Copper Study Group. Copper prices on the London Metal Exchange have climbed above $14,500 per metric ton, reaching record highs.

The industry's challenge of managing public perception while simultaneously expanding infrastructure at speed is likely to remain a central tension heading into the final months of 2026, particularly as community-level opposition to data center siting shows no sign of abating.
