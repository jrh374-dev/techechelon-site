Germany's far-right Alternative for Germany party claimed a historic first-place finish in Saxony-Anhalt on Sunday, capturing 44.5% of the vote in state elections, according to exit polls aired on the ARD broadcaster — though the party's ability to translate that result into actual governance remained far from certain.

The exit poll placed Chancellor Friedrich Merz's conservative CDU a distant second at 18.5%, while the center-left Social Democrats and the environmental Greens both cleared the minimum parliamentary threshold.

AfD co-leader Alice Weidel declared the result "historic" and argued it gave her party a clear mandate to govern Saxony-Anhalt.

The AfD is led in the state by Ulrich Siegmund, a 35-year-old who cast his ballot Sunday in his hometown of Tangermünde, arriving on a blue Simson moped to crowds of supporters. Siegmund has ruled out coalition talks with other parties, meaning the AfD would need an outright majority to enter government — a bar that the exit poll figure alone does not guarantee depending on final seat allocations.

Germany's domestic intelligence service has investigated the AfD for right-wing extremism, and the party has been formally classified as "far-right extremist" by the state security service. Mainstream parties have responded by maintaining a so-called "firewall" — a collective refusal to enter coalition arrangements with the AfD at any level of government. The AfD characterizes such accusations as scaremongering and an insult to its supporters.

The only party that has not ruled out a coalition with the AfD is the small left-wing populist Sahra Wagenknecht Alliance, known as BSW — but exit poll data suggested BSW also risks falling short of the threshold required to enter parliament.

If the AfD falls short of an outright governing majority and BSW is absent from parliament, the result could force the remaining mainstream parties into protracted coalition negotiations. One scenario under discussion involves a minority government led by current state incumbent Schulze, dependent on passive tolerance from the Left Party — a model already in use in the neighboring eastern German state of Thuringia.

The Free Democratic Party, one of Merz's federal coalition partners, is also projected to miss the parliamentary threshold in Saxony-Anhalt, while the SPD sits below 10% in polling.

The result is the latest setback for Merz, whose national approval ratings have declined as his coalition government contends with sluggish economic growth in Europe's largest economy. Nationally, the AfD has risen to become Germany's most popular party, even as the firewall among other parties remains officially intact.

AfD officials have framed Sunday's Saxony-Anhalt election as a proving ground for the party's broader ambitions ahead of the next federal election, scheduled for 2029. Should the party enter government in the state, it has said it would push schools and cultural institutions toward more traditional values, tighten immigration policy, and overhaul public broadcasting.

Germany's Nazi-era history has long shaped a particular wariness among mainstream political actors toward far-right movements, distinguishing the country's political dynamics from those of other European nations where hard-right parties have made similar gains. Whether that wariness holds as the AfD's electoral results continue to climb will be one of the defining questions facing German democracy in the years ahead.
