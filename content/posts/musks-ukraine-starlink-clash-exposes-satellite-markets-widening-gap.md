Tensions between Elon Musk and Ukraine over restricted satellite internet service have sharpened scrutiny of the world's near-total dependence on SpaceX's Starlink and its military-grade Starshield offering — and underscored how far rival providers remain from filling that gap.

On Oct. 14, Ukrainian officials accused SpaceX of suddenly and without warning imposing speed limits on Starshield, the company's secured satellite network for government entities. The changes cut wireless internet connectivity in drones and other aircraft moving at speeds above 100 mph that Ukrainian forces were using against Russia. A Ukrainian senior commander said the restrictions cost his unit more than $1 million and delayed missions by three days.

The Wall Street Journal reported on Tuesday that Musk individually ordered the service changes and had discussed them with President Donald Trump before putting them in place. Trump told reporters Wednesday that he "was not involved."

The episode has drawn bipartisan condemnation in Congress. Sen. John Cornyn, R-Texas, told CNBC that he does not believe Musk would have acted "without at least the acquiescence of the White House," and called attempts to throttle Starlink service in Ukraine "frankly shameful." Rep. Pramila Jayapal, D-Wash., called for a formal investigation, writing that "no billionaire, especially one that gets massive contracts from our government, should have the power to unilaterally decide to shut down communications and weapons during a war of critical importance to the US and Ukraine."

Robert Weissman, co-president of watchdog group Public Citizen, described the situation as an "extreme danger" that raises antitrust and national security concerns. "People worry about the political, as well as economic, impacts of oligarchy, and this is it," Weissman said. "No person should have that ability or power."

On SpaceX's earnings call this week, executives did not address the Starshield restrictions and were not asked about them by analysts. SpaceX COO Gwynne Shotwell said Tuesday that Starlink connectivity is now live in 174 countries, with more than 11,000 satellites in orbit, according to data tracked by astronomer Jonathan C. McDowell.

That scale dwarfs every competitor currently in the field. Amazon's Project Kuiper, a low Earth orbit constellation seven years in the making, has only 390 satellites in orbit and has yet to fully come online. France's Eutelsat has more than 600 satellites in orbit, and its stock surged 23% on Oct. 14 as reports of the Musk-Ukraine dispute circulated — though independent satellite consultant Christian Freiherr von der Ropp noted that the cost, size, weight, and power draw of Eutelsat's terminals make them "particularly unsuitable in the battlefield."

Blue Origin, Jeff Bezos' space company, unveiled an enterprise and government service called TeraWave in January and is targeting its first satellite deployment in the fourth quarter of 2027. The European Union's IRIS² sovereign constellation, expected to span hundreds of satellites, will not see its first launches until 2029. Most of these planned systems are unlikely to "come anywhere close" in terms of capacity, coverage, resilience, or cost compared to Starlink, von der Ropp said.

The competitive landscape is further complicated by SpaceX's dominance of the launch market. The company has carried satellites to orbit for many of its rivals, and is now phasing out commercial rides on its Falcon 9 rocket as it develops the larger Starship. New constellations "could therefore face substantial delays and materially higher launch costs," von der Ropp said.

The episode signals that the strategic vulnerability exposed in Ukraine is unlikely to be resolved quickly. With the nearest credible alternative still years from matching Starlink's scale, governments seeking independent satellite communications capacity face a long and expensive road — one that SpaceX's own launch business may help shape on its own terms.
