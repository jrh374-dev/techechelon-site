The Commerce Department moved Monday to close a regulatory gap that had allowed Chinese companies to lease advanced computing power built on Nvidia chips through data centers in third countries, the first formal U.S. policy response to the scandal that led Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang to resign last week.

The action, an interim final rule issued by the Bureau of Industry and Security, came after mounting criticism of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick over his handling of potential violations of American export controls.

The department's press release noted that the BIS rule was "written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council," a formulation that pointedly attributed authorship to agencies other than Commerce.

The rule targets what officials describe as a structural vulnerability: foreign actors using computing infrastructure located outside China — but powered by restricted U.S. semiconductors — to train advanced artificial intelligence models.

"The Department of Commerce is establishing new rules to prevent certain foreign actors from leveraging advanced computing power based on U.S. technology to threaten U.S. national security and foreign policy interests," the interim final rule states.

The rule adds that the restrictions "will undermine the ability of companies headquartered in countries of concern from using U.S. advanced Super Intelligence (SI) semiconductors to train advanced SI models."

Under the new framework, a license will be required to export, reexport, or transfer advanced semiconductors to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, or Brazil — countries the rule describes as presenting "a higher-than-normal risk of diversion." The Commerce Secretary may also designate additional countries in consultation with the Secretary of State or the National Security Council.

Singapore-based Megaspeed, identified as the company at the center of the Nvidia scandal, became a flashpoint between cabinet departments last week. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, in a Friday interview on CNBC's "Squawk Box," blamed Commerce for failing to place Megaspeed on the entity list, which would have restricted exports to the firm.

Nvidia shares fell 2.2% Monday, extending steep losses from the prior week, as traders also braced for the company's fiscal third-quarter results due after the market close Tuesday. Analysts at UBS and Mizuho each trimmed their price targets on the chipmaker ahead of the report.

The sell-off extended to other AI-linked names. Micron Technology, Intel, and Advanced Micro Devices all declined following the announcement of the interim final rule.

The broader technology and risk-asset environment remained under pressure Monday. Bitcoin fell as low as $62,000 late Saturday — its lowest level since August — before recovering to around $64,000 by Monday morning, according to Coin Metrics data, logging its sixth consecutive weekly decline. On Saturday night alone, exchanges tracked by CoinGlass recorded more than $165 million in liquidated bitcoin long positions, following $629 million on Friday and nearly $700 million on Thursday.

Nansen research analyst Nicolai Sondergaard said bitcoin's next critical test sits between $60,000 and $62,000. "A break below $60,000 is possible if Nvidia's results add to the tech selling," he said.

The Nvidia earnings report Tuesday now carries weight well beyond the chipmaker's own balance sheet — with investors, policymakers, and crypto markets all watching for signals about the trajectory of AI spending and the broader technology sector amid an escalating regulatory crackdown.
