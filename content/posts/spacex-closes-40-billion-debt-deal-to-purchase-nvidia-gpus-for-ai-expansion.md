SpaceX has closed $40 billion in debt financing to purchase graphics processing units used to power artificial intelligence systems, Elon Musk announced Sunday on X, underscoring the enormous capital demands of the AI infrastructure buildout.

Musk said the financing consisted primarily of callable debt, with the GPUs expected to arrive in the first and second quarters of 2027. He added that SpaceX carries investment-grade credit ratings and generates substantial revenue from its Starlink satellite internet and rocket launch operations.

The deal had been in the works for some time. Goldman Sachs was leading talks with investors, including Apollo Global Management, over the financing structure, according to earlier reporting. A special purpose vehicle was proposed to keep the debt off SpaceX's balance sheet and preserve its investment-grade ratings, people familiar with the matter said.

The transaction comes as SpaceX has moved aggressively into artificial intelligence this year, branding the segment "Super Intelligence," or SI. The unit has grown into the company's largest business, with third-quarter revenue rising ninefold to $7.56 billion from $813 million in the year-earlier period, according to SpaceX's latest earnings release.

Musk has publicly committed SpaceX to using Nvidia chips exclusively, making the company one of the most committed buyers of the chipmaker's GPUs among major technology players. The financing locks in a significant supply of that hardware ahead of anticipated demand in 2027.

In the same post, Musk offered a pointed assessment of conditions in the broader AI infrastructure market, warning that debt financing has largely become inaccessible for companies that lack substantial revenue outside AI data centers.

He predicted that more than 200 so-called neocloud companies — providers of cloud computing infrastructure built specifically for AI and machine learning workloads — would face widespread failures and consolidation, with only a small number surviving.

The warning reflects deepening stress in a sector that has relied heavily on borrowed capital to fuel rapid expansion. CoreWeave, one of the largest and most visible neocloud operators, has seen its high-yield debt come under pressure, raising concerns about the broader ability of undercapitalized infrastructure firms to service their obligations as interest rates remain elevated.

Nvidia has maintained close financial relationships with several neocloud companies through equity stakes and partnerships, a model some analysts have characterized as circular — with Nvidia-backed firms using that support to purchase Nvidia hardware.

SpaceX's ability to close the financing, even as conditions tighten for smaller rivals, reflects the company's differentiated position: a diversified revenue base that lenders view as sufficient collateral for debt of this scale. Most neocloud operators lack comparable revenue outside their AI data center businesses.

What happens to the neocloud sector over the next several quarters will likely determine how much of the current AI chip buildout translates into durable infrastructure, and how much becomes a casualty of the credit tightening Musk described.
